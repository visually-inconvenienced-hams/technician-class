Lesson 1: What ham radio is and what this course will do for you

Welcome to the class! We're glad you're here. This course teaches you about ham radio one small step at a time. You don't need any past skill with radio to start. Each idea comes in plain words. And there's a short quiz after each lesson, so you can check what you've learned.

What you will learn

  • What amateur radio is, and what it is not.
  • What a license lets you do.
  • How this course works.
  • What the exam asks for.
  • How to study with the group.

The idea in plain words

Ham radio is a hobby. It's all about people talking to each other by radio — and learning how radio works along the way. It's not for business use. The people who do it are called hams. You'll find them chatting with friends nearby, reaching strangers far away, and even talking to the space station. Hams also help out in storms and floods. They build their own radios and antennas, and they keep learning how radio waves work.

You need a license to send on amateur radio. A license is a grant from the Federal Communications Commission, the FCC for short. The FCC makes the rules for radio in the United States. Your license gives you a call sign. That's the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air. You say your call sign when you talk, so other hams know who you are.

The FCC lists five goals for ham radio. One is to help the public in an emergency. A second is to push the radio art ahead. A third is to build skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art. A fourth is to grow a pool of trained operators. A fifth is to build good will with other lands.

The Technician license is the first license, and this course gets you ready for it. You earn it by passing one exam. The exam has 35 questions. Each one has 4 choices, with only one right answer. You pass with 26 right. The questions come from a public list of 409 questions. This course teaches you the ideas behind them. So you're learning how things work, not just memorizing answers.

A Technician license lets you talk on local bands. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. A frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. Most new hams start on the 2 meter band, where the national call channel is 146.52 megahertz (MHz). A megahertz is one million hertz, and one hertz is one repeat per second.

Local hams use repeaters to reach farther. A repeater is a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. So you can talk through one with a small hand radio. You can also join the group net on Saturday nights. It meets at 19:00 Central on Saturdays, on the Blind Hams Bridge.

This course has 35 lessons, and each one has a short quiz. Each lesson takes about 10 minutes to read or hear. The lessons live right here in this group, so you study with other members. You can ask questions in the group any time. No lesson needs a picture. Each term is defined right where you meet it.

Ham radio fits many lives. Some hams talk for a few minutes each day, some build big stations, and some chase far contacts. Some serve their town in storms. You pick what fits you — this course just gives you the start.

The exam tests ideas, not tricks. Each question has one right choice. The list of 409 questions is public, and you can read all of them. This course covers each idea in the list. The quizzes use real questions from it.

And you're not alone in this class. Other members are studying right along with you. You can share what you learn and help each other as you go.

An example

Think of a new ham named Ana. She joins this group and reads one lesson each day. Then she takes the short quiz after each lesson. When a term is new to her, she asks about it in the group. Ana uses a screen reader, and the lessons work with one. So nothing in the course leaves her out. After 35 lessons, she takes some practice exams. Then she passes her real exam and gets her call sign. The next Saturday, she joins the net and talks to hams across town. Her very first contact is with a ham in her own town. Ana feels proud of her new skill, and she should. You can do the same.

What to remember

  • Amateur radio is for people, not for business.
  • One goal of ham radio is to build skills in the radio art.
  • A license lets you send on the air.
  • The Technician license is the first step.
  • The exam has 35 questions, and you pass with 26 right.
  • This course has 35 lessons, each with a short quiz.
  • You study here, in the group.

Words to know

  • band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.
  • call sign: the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air.
  • amateur radio (ham radio): radio that people use to talk to each other and to learn about radio. It is not for business use.
  • frequency: how many times a radio wave repeats in one second.
  • megahertz (MHz): one million hertz.
  • hertz (Hz): the unit for frequency. One hertz is one repeat per second.
  • repeater: a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • license: a grant from the FCC that lets you send on amateur radio.
  • FCC (Federal Communications Commission): the agency that makes the rules for radio in the United States.
  • radio art: the craft of radio.

Lesson 2: License classes, your call sign, and how long it lasts

Welcome back. In lesson 1 you met ham radio and saw how this course works. Now let's look at the license itself. You'll learn the three classes, how your call sign works, and how long it lasts.

What you will learn

  • The 3 license classes, and which one you get first.
  • How you get a call sign.
  • How long a license lasts, and how you renew it.
  • What to do if your license ends.

The idea in plain words

You need a license before you send signals on the ham bands. A license is a permit from the FCC. The FCC is the Federal Communications Commission. It makes the rules for radio in the United States, and it gives out ham licenses. No license, no sending — that part is simple.

The FCC gives out 3 kinds of ham license: Technician, General, and Amateur Extra. Technician is the first class, and it's the one this course gets you ready for. General and Amateur Extra need more tests. Each higher class lets you use more parts of the bands. A band, by the way, is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. The FCC no longer gives out Novice, Advanced, or Technician Plus licenses. So no new ham gets one of those old classes.

A Technician can do a lot. You can talk on local repeaters. A repeater is a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. You can talk to the space station on VHF bands. VHF is very high frequency, from 30 to 300 megahertz. You can also use voice on the 10-meter band. That's the only HF band where a Technician may use voice. Your voice range on 10 meters is 28.300 to 28.500 megahertz. Some beacons send on their own, with no person at the radio. These propagation beacons live on 10 meters only, from 28.200 to 28.300 megahertz. HF is high frequency, from 3 to 30 megahertz.

Hams also follow a band plan. A band plan is a voluntary guideline for using different modes or activities within an amateur band. It goes beyond the privileges the FCC sets.

Your license comes with a call sign. That's the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air. You say it on the air so other hams know who you are. A call sign has letters, a number, and more letters. KF1XXX is one example of a Technician call sign. It starts with 2 letters, then comes the number 1, and then 3 more letters.

You get your call sign when you pass the test. But there's one more step first. Your license grant has to show in the FCC license database. A grant is the FCC record that says you hold a license. One grant covers both you and your station. It's an operator license and a station license in one. You may send as soon as your grant shows in the database. You may not send before that time, even though you've passed. The database is the proof: if your name shows there, you hold a license. The FCC also sends you an email with a link to get a copy of your license.

You can also pick your own call sign. A call sign you pick is called a vanity call sign. Any licensed ham may ask for one.

Your license lasts 10 years. You can ask to renew it up to 90 days before it ends. Renewal costs 35 dollars, and the new license lasts 10 more years. You don't take a test to renew. If your license ends, you still have 2 more years to renew it. Those 2 years are called the grace period — that's extra time to renew after a license ends. But you must not send while your license has ended. You have to wait until the FCC renews it.

You may operate from a US-documented vessel in international waters, with the master's permission. A club can hold a station license too. To get one, the club must have at least four members.

One more thing: keep your email address up to date with the FCC. If the FCC can't reach you by email, you can lose your license. So check your email address each time you move, and when you get a new email address too.

An example

Maria passes her Technician test on a Saturday. On the next Tuesday, her grant shows in the FCC license database. From that moment, she can send on the ham bands. Her call sign has letters, a number, and more letters. She writes it on a card by her radio, so it's always in reach. She says it when the rules say she must. Lesson 3 will teach her when that is. She also gets an email from the FCC, with a link to a copy of her license. She saves the copy on her computer. Ten years go by. Then, 90 days before her license ends, Maria asks to renew it. She pays 35 dollars and takes no test. Her new license lasts 10 more years.

What to remember

  • There are 3 license classes: Technician, General, and Amateur Extra.
  • Technician is the first class.
  • On 10 meters, Technician voice is 28.300 to 28.500 megahertz.
  • A club station license needs at least four members.
  • Your call sign is your name on the air.
  • A license lasts 10 years.
  • You can renew up to 90 days before it ends.
  • The grace period is 2 years, but you must not send while your license has ended.
  • Keep your email address up to date with the FCC.

Words to know

  • License: a permit from the FCC that lets you send signals on the ham bands.
  • Call sign: the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air.
  • Band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.
  • Repeater: a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • VHF (very high frequency): radio frequencies from 30 to 300 megahertz.
  • HF (high frequency): radio frequencies from 3 to 30 megahertz.
  • Grant: the FCC record that says you hold a license.
  • Vanity call sign: a call sign you pick yourself.
  • Grace period: extra time to renew a license after it ends.
  • Band plan: a voluntary guideline for using different modes or activities within an amateur band.
  • Propagation beacon: a station that sends on its own so hams can test how well signals travel.

Lesson 3: Station rules: who can use your radio, and how you identify

Welcome back. Lesson 2 was about your license. This time, let's talk about life on the air. You'll learn when to say your call sign, and who may use your radio.

What you will learn

  • When to say your call sign on the air.
  • How to say your call sign.
  • Who may use your radio station.
  • What a control operator does and who picks one.

The idea in plain words

You must say your call sign on the air. Say it at least once each 10 minutes while you talk. Say it again at the end of your last send. This rule holds for each contact. It holds when you test your radio too. A test send must still name your station. One case needs no call sign. You may send with no call sign when you send signals to control a model craft.

Say your call sign in English when you use voice. Voice is also called phone. You may say it by voice, or you may send it in Morse code. Morse code is a code of short and long signals that stand for letters. A phonetic alphabet can help. That's a set of standard words for letters, such as Alfa for A. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) urges hams to use it when they say a call sign by voice.

You may add a second call sign after your own. You join the 2 parts with one word — stroke, slant, or slash. All 3 words are fine.

Some hams use a job name on the air. One example is Race Headquarters. A job name is called a tactical call sign. But you still must say your FCC call sign each 10 minutes and at the end. The job name does not take the place of your call sign.

Each station has a control operator. That's the licensed person who is in charge of a station when it transmits. To transmit is to send a radio signal. The station licensee picks the control operator. The licensee is the person whose name is on the station license. A station must never send with no control operator. The control operator makes sure the station follows the rules.

The control operator's license class sets what the station may do. A Technician control operator may use Technician bands only. So a Technician can never run a station in an Amateur Extra part of the bands. The one exception is a true emergency. Lesson 4 will cover emergencies.

If the control operator is not the licensee, both must answer for how the station runs. The control operator is in charge. The licensee shares the blame if the station breaks a rule.

The control point is the place where the control operator does the job. It can be at the radio, or it can be far from the radio. A ham can run a station over the internet, and that is called remote control. Any station may be run by remote control.

Some stations run on their own. A repeater is one example. A repeater is a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. A repeater runs under automatic control. That means no person sits at the radio. But a repeater still has a control operator.

A person with no license may speak on your station. But you must be the control operator, and you must stay in charge while they speak. You must make sure they follow the rules. A message sent for someone else is called third-party communication. That is a message sent for a person who is not the control operator.

A guest may speak to a ham in another country. But that country must have a third-party agreement with the United States. If it has no such agreement, the guest may not speak to that station.

Talk with hams in other lands has two rules. Your talk must be incidental to the purposes of ham radio, and remarks of a personal character. You must not talk with a ham in a country whose administration has told the ITU that it objects. The ITU is the International Telecommunication Union.

One last rule. You must show your station to the FCC when asked, and your station records too. An FCC official may ask at any time.

An example

Tom holds a Technician license, and his friend Ana has no license. Ana wants to greet a ham in another country. So Tom sits at the radio as the control operator. First he checks that the country has a third-party agreement with the United States. It does, so Ana may speak. She speaks into the microphone while Tom stays in charge the whole time. Tom says his call sign each 10 minutes, and once more at the end. If Ana says the wrong thing, Tom must stop her. Tom answers for all that Ana says on the air.

What to remember

  • Say your call sign each 10 minutes and at the end.
  • Use English when you say your call sign by voice.
  • A station must never send with no control operator.
  • The licensee picks the control operator.
  • The control operator's class sets what the station may do.
  • A guest with no license may speak if you stay in charge.
  • A guest may speak to a foreign ham only with a third-party agreement.
  • You may send with no call sign to control a model craft.
  • Do not talk with a country that told the ITU it objects.

Words to know

  • Transmit: to send a radio signal.
  • Call sign: the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air.
  • Control operator: the licensed person who is in charge of a station when it transmits.
  • Station licensee: the person whose name is on the station license.
  • Control point: the place where the control operator does the job.
  • Third-party communication: a message sent for a person who is not the control operator.
  • Phonetic alphabet: standard words for letters, such as Alfa for A.
  • Tactical call sign: a job name used on the air, such as Race Headquarters.
  • Morse code: a code of short and long signals that stand for letters.
  • Remote control: running a station from a place away from the radio.
  • Automatic control: running a station with no person at the radio.
  • Repeater: a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • Model craft: a small model plane, boat, or car that you control by radio.
  • ITU: the world group that sets rules for radio use.

Lesson 4: Power, interference, and emergencies

Welcome back. Lesson 3 covered station rules and how you identify. Now let's talk about power, interference, and emergencies.

What you will learn

  • How much power a Technician may use.
  • What harmful interference is and how to avoid it.
  • What the rules allow when lives are at risk.
  • Which sends are never allowed.

The idea in plain words

Power is the rate at which electric energy is used. The watt is the unit for power. The limit for a radio is peak envelope power. That's the most power a signal reaches at its peaks. A Technician may use up to 200 watts on HF bands. HF is high frequency, from 3 to 30 megahertz. A megahertz means one million hertz. One hertz is one repeat per second. On frequencies above 30 megahertz, a Technician may use up to 1500 watts.

Harmful interference is a signal that stops another station from working. You must not cause it on purpose. Willful or malicious interference is banned. Willful means on purpose. Malicious means meant to harm. Keep your station in good shape. A station that works right causes less trouble for others.

Some band parts are shared with other users. In those parts, ham radio is second in line. You must not block them.

Don't set your radio right at the edge of a band. Your signal can spill past the edge. Your signal may drift a bit too. So stay a small step inside the edge.

Two stations on one frequency may clash. When that happens, both should talk it out. They should agree on how to share it.

Your station may bother a neighbor's radio or TV. First check your own house. Make sure your station works right. Make sure it does not bother your own radio or TV on the same channel. A device in a neighbor's home may bother your station. Work with your neighbor. Find the device that causes the trouble. Then tell your neighbor the FCC rules ban devices that cause interference. Make sure your own station meets good practice.

Radio frequency interference has three causes. One is fundamental overload. That is when a receiver cannot reject a strong signal outside its own band. The others are harmonics and spurious emissions. A home AM or FM radio can pick up your signal this way. To cut that interference, block your signal with a filter. Put the filter at the antenna input of the affected receiver. A band-reject filter can cut interference to your 2-meter radio from a nearby commercial FM station. If your signal bothers cable TV, check the cables first. Be sure all TV feed line coaxial connectors are installed properly.

Some sends are never allowed. You must not broadcast — broadcasting is a send meant for the general public. Broadcasting is the banned kind of one-way transmission. You must not play music. The one exception is music that comes with a relay of a manned spacecraft. You must not use indecent or obscene language. You must not send secret codes. Codes are allowed only to control a space station or a model craft.

You may tell hams about gear for sale or trade. The gear must be amateur radio equipment. And you must not do this on a regular basis. A control operator may take pay for one kind of work. That work is classroom instruction at an educational institution. You may send news for a broadcast station only in one case. The news must directly relate to the immediate safety of human life. Or it must relate to the protection of property. No other means may be at hand.

The FCC rules always apply. They apply in an emergency too. But the rules give you one special right then. In a true emergency, you may use a frequency your license does not cover. You may do this only when human life or property is in direct danger. Direct danger means now, not soon. A storm on the way is not direct danger. A person hurt now is direct danger. When the danger ends, you must go back to your own bands.

Two groups help in a crisis. RACES is the Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service. It's an FCC amateur radio service for civil defense during national emergencies. To be the control operator of a RACES station, you need proof. A civil defense group must have enrolled you. ARES is the Amateur Radio Emergency Service. It's a group of licensed hams.

A net is a group of stations. They meet on one frequency at a set time. A net control station runs the net. It calls the net to order. It directs who speaks and when. In a net, speak only when the net control station tells you to. The one exception is an emergency. If you report an emergency, you may speak at once. Nets also pass formal notes called traffic. Traffic is formal notes passed from one station to the next. Each formal message, or radiogram, has a preamble. The preamble holds the information needed to track the message. Each message also has a check. The check counts the words or word equivalents in the text part.

An example

A big storm hits your town. The phones are dead. A neighbor needs an ambulance fast. You're a Technician. The only station you can reach is on a frequency you may not use. But the rules give you a way. A life is in direct danger. So you may use that frequency to call for help. You say your call sign. You ask for help. When help is on the way, you leave it. You go back to your own bands.

What to remember

  • A Technician may use up to 200 watts on HF.
  • Above 30 megahertz, a Technician may use up to 1500 watts.
  • Never cause interference on purpose.
  • Stay inside the band edges.
  • In an emergency, you may use any frequency when a life or property is in direct danger.
  • The FCC rules always apply, even in an emergency.
  • RACES and ARES are 2 groups that help in a crisis.

Words to know

  • Watt: the unit for power.
  • Power: the rate at which electric energy is used.
  • Peak envelope power: the most power a signal reaches at its peaks.
  • HF (high frequency): radio frequencies from 3 to 30 megahertz.
  • Megahertz (MHz): one million hertz.
  • Hertz (Hz): the unit for frequency. One hertz is one repeat per second.
  • Harmful interference: a signal that stops another station from working.
  • Broadcasting: a send meant for the general public.
  • RACES: the Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service, an FCC service for civil defense in national emergencies.
  • ARES: the Amateur Radio Emergency Service, a group of licensed hams who give their time and gear to help the public.
  • Net: a group of stations that meet on one frequency at a set time.
  • Net control station: the station that runs a net.
  • Traffic: formal notes passed from one station to the next in a net.
  • Control operator: the licensed person who is in charge of a station when it transmits.

Lesson 5: Repeaters: how they work and how you use one

Welcome back. Lesson 4 covered power, interference, and emergencies. Now let's meet the repeater. It's the helper that lets a small hand radio reach across town.

What you will learn

  • What a repeater does.
  • What an offset is.
  • What a tone is.
  • How to make a contact through a repeater.

The idea in plain words

A repeater helps your signal go far. It's a station on a high place. It hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. It sits on a tall tower or on top of a high building. Your small radio can't reach far on its own. Its signal is weak, and hills and buildings block it. But the repeater hears your weak signal. It sends your voice out again with more power. So other hams can hear you from far away.

The picture with this lesson shows how a repeater works. On the left, a ham holds a small radio. Curved lines go up from the radio to a tall tower, with the repeater at the top. More curved lines go down from the tower to a second ham on the right. The lines going up are the input signal, and the lines going down are the output signal. So the picture shows the signal going up weak and coming down strong.

A repeater listens on one frequency and talks on a second frequency. A frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. The frequency you send on is the input frequency. The frequency the repeater sends on is the output frequency. The gap between the two is the offset. You set your radio to send on the input frequency. You set it to listen on the output frequency. Your radio does the shift for you when you set the offset.

Most new hams start on the 2-meter band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. On the 2-meter band, the offset is plus or minus 600 kilohertz (kHz). One kilohertz is one thousand hertz. One hertz is one repeat per second. The plus sign means the input is above the output. The minus sign means the input is below the output. Your radio can set this offset for you. You only need to pick plus or minus. On the 70-centimeter band, the offset is plus or minus 5 megahertz.

Your radio may have a reverse button. The reverse function lets you listen on a repeater's input frequency.

Many repeaters need a tone to open. A tone is a low sound, too low to hear, that opens a repeater. The full name is Continuous Tone-Coded Squelch System, or CTCSS for short. You set the tone in your radio. Your radio sends the tone with your voice. You can't hear the tone. The repeater hears it and opens. Without the right tone, the repeater stays shut. If you hear a repeater but cannot open it, check three things. Your offset, your CTCSS tone, or your DCS code may be wrong. DCS is Digital-Coded Squelch, a code that opens a repeater. The tone opens the squelch of the repeater. Squelch is a switch that keeps a radio quiet until a real signal comes in. To hear a weak FM signal, set the squelch threshold so that receiver output audio is on all the time. You can find the tone in a repeater list. Many hams use a list on the web or in a book.

You can also talk without a repeater. That is called simplex, which means talking and listening on the same frequency. Simplex works well when the other station is near. A repeater works better when the other station is far. Band plans set aside simplex channels in the VHF and UHF bands. They let stations within range of each other talk without tying up a repeater.

Repeaters must not clash with each other. A Volunteer Frequency Coordinator recommends transmit and receive channels. These channels are for repeater and auxiliary stations. Local amateurs recognize the coordinator. Amateurs in a local or regional area select the coordinator. Their stations must be eligible to be repeater or auxiliary stations. An auxiliary station sends one-way transmissions. They run between a remote repeater receiver and the main repeater transmitter. A repeater may pass on a rule-breaking send by mistake. Then the control operator of the originating station is accountable. The repeater is not to blame.

An example

Say you're driving home from work. You want to talk to your friend Sam. Sam lives on the far side of town. Your small radio can't reach that far on its own. So you use a repeater. You tune your radio to the repeater output frequency. Then set the offset to minus 600 kilohertz. Then you set the tone the repeater needs. Then you listen for a short time. No one is talking. So you press the talk button. Say Sam's call sign, then say your call sign. You let go of the button and listen. You hear a short beep. That's the courtesy tone. It tells you the repeater is ready. Sam hears the repeater and answers you. You talk in short turns. When you're done, you say your call sign. The repeater helped your signal go far.

What to remember

  • A repeater hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • The offset is the gap between the input frequency and the output frequency.
  • On the 2-meter band, the offset is plus or minus 600 kilohertz.
  • On the 70-centimeter band, the offset is plus or minus 5 megahertz.
  • The reverse function lets you listen on a repeater's input frequency.
  • A tone opens the repeater.
  • Listen first, then call, and keep each turn short.
  • Say your call sign at the start and at the end.

Words to know

  • repeater: a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • offset: the gap between a repeater's input frequency and its output frequency.
  • frequency: how many times a radio wave repeats in one second.
  • kilohertz: one thousand hertz.
  • hertz: the unit for frequency. One hertz is one repeat per second.
  • input frequency: the frequency you send on to reach the repeater.
  • output frequency: the frequency the repeater sends on.
  • simplex: talking and listening on the same frequency.
  • squelch: a switch that keeps a radio quiet until a real signal comes in.
  • CTCSS: Continuous Tone-Coded Squelch System. It is the tone that opens a repeater.
  • call sign: the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air.
  • band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.
  • courtesy tone: a short beep that tells you the repeater is ready.

Lesson 6: Making contacts: calling, answering, and good manners on the air

Welcome back. Lesson 5 was about repeaters. Now comes the fun part: making a contact and talking to another ham.

What you will learn

  • What CQ means.
  • How to call CQ.
  • How to answer a call.
  • Good manners on the air.
  • What Q signals mean.

The idea in plain words

A contact is a talk between two hams by radio. You make a contact when you call and a ham answers you. A contact can last a few minutes or a long time. You can call one ham by name, or you can call any ham who hears you. That open call is CQ, which means calling any station. Hams have used CQ for more than 100 years — it's a short way to say you want a talk.

Here's how you call CQ. First, listen to the frequency and make sure no one else is using it. A frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. Then transmit your call — to transmit means to send your voice out by radio. Say CQ three times, then say your call sign. Say your call sign two times, then listen for an answer. If no one answers, call again.

Here's how you answer a call. Listen for the call sign of the ham who calls, then transmit. Say the other ham's call sign first, then say your call sign. That is the right order, so the other ham knows you're calling them. Say your call sign two times if the signal is weak, then listen for the other ham to answer you.

On a repeater, the call works the same way. To call a ham whose call sign you know, say their call sign first, then say yours. To show you want a talk, say your call sign followed by the word "listening".

Hams also use short codes called Q signals. A Q signal is three letters that start with Q. Hams made them up long ago for Morse code, to save time. Hams still use them on voice too. Each Q signal can be a question or a statement. With a question mark, it asks. Without one, it tells.

Here are the ones you will hear most. QRM means interference from other stations. If another ham's signal covers up the one you want to hear, that is QRM. QSY means change frequency. If two hams want a clear spot, one says, "Let's QSY up five." That means move up five kilohertz. QTH means location. "What is your QTH?" asks where you are. QSL means I confirm what you sent. Hams also send QSL cards to confirm a contact. QRZ means who is calling me. If you hear a call but miss who called, say "QRZ?" and the ham will call again.

Two of these are on your exam. QRM is the answer when the test asks which Q signal means interference from other stations. QSY is the answer when the test asks which Q signal means changing frequency. Learn them both.

Good manners keep the bands friendly. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. Ham radio is not for business use. Always listen before you transmit, and don't talk over another ham. Keep your turns short, and give other hams a chance to join in. Say your call sign when you start and when you stop. Speak in a clear, calm voice, use plain words, and say what you mean. Don't use rude words, and don't play music on the air. Thank the other ham when you finish — many hams end with 73, which means best wishes.

Spell your call sign with the phonetic alphabet when the other ham can't hear it well. The phonetic alphabet is a set of standard words for letters, such as Alfa for A. It helps when the signal is weak. Say Alfa for A, say Bravo for B, and say each word slow and clear. You can also give a signal report, which tells the other ham how well you hear them. Say if the voice is loud and clear, or if it's weak. Keep the report short and true.

On the 2-meter band, hams call on 146.52 megahertz (MHz). One megahertz is one million hertz, and one hertz is one repeat per second. That is the national simplex calling frequency — simplex means talking and listening on the same frequency. Many hams meet new friends on 146.52 megahertz: call there, then move to a new frequency to talk.

An example

You sit at your radio at home. It's 19:00, and you want to make a contact. You tune to 146.52 megahertz and listen for a short time. No one is talking. So you say CQ three times and your call sign two times. Then you listen. A ham answers you. She says your call sign first, then her call sign. You answer back the same way: her call sign first, then yours. You talk for a short time, and you tell her your name and your town. She tells you her name and her town. Then you thank her for the contact, say 73, and say your call sign. That was your first contact.

  • Keep your turns short and give others a chance to join in.
  • Say your call sign when you start and when you stop.
  • Use the phonetic alphabet when the other ham cannot hear your call sign well.
  • End with 73, which means best wishes.

  • megahertz: one million hertz.

Lesson 7: Radio waves: frequency and wavelength

Welcome back. Lesson 6 was about contacts. Now let's look at what carries your voice from one radio to another: the radio wave itself.

What you will learn

  • What a radio wave is.
  • What frequency and wavelength mean.
  • How frequency and wavelength link.
  • How fast a radio wave travels.
  • How hams name bands.
  • How to find wavelength from frequency.

The idea in plain words

A radio wave is a wave of energy that you cannot see. Your radio makes radio waves. An antenna is the part of your station that sends and receives radio waves. Your antenna sends the waves out into the air, and another antenna picks them up far away. That is how your voice travels from one radio to another.

A radio wave has two parts: an electric field and a magnetic field. The two fields sit at right angles to each other. The direction, or orientation, of the electric field sets the wave's polarization.

A radio wave moves at the speed of light. That speed is 300 million meters per second. All radio waves move at the same speed, low or high frequency.

Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. The unit for frequency is the hertz, and one hertz is one repeat per second. One thousand hertz is one kilohertz, and one million hertz is one megahertz. Hams write megahertz as MHz, and you'll see MHz on your radio.

Wavelength is the distance from one peak of a wave to the next peak. A peak is the top of a wave. Frequency and wavelength link in a fixed way. When frequency goes up, wavelength gets shorter. When frequency goes down, wavelength gets longer.

Hams group frequencies into bands. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. Each band has a name from its wavelength. The 2 meter band uses 146.52 megahertz (MHz), and its waves are about 2 meters long. The 20 meter band uses about 14 megahertz (MHz), and its waves are about 21 meters long.

You can find the wavelength with a simple rule. Divide 300 by the frequency in megahertz, and the answer is the wavelength in meters. Take 146.52 megahertz (MHz) as an example: 300 divided by 146.52 is about 2. So the wavelength is about 2 meters.

The diagram with this lesson shows two waves side by side. The wave on the left has a few wide peaks that sit far apart. Its label says low frequency and long wavelength. The wave on the right has many narrow peaks that sit close together. Its label says high frequency and short wavelength. A line with arrows marks the gap from one peak to the next peak on each wave. The gap is long on the left wave and short on the right wave. You do not need the picture to learn this fact.

An example

You want to know how long the waves are on the 2 meter band. You look at your radio, and it says 146.52 megahertz (MHz). You divide 300 by 146.52, and you get about 2. So the waves are about 2 meters long. That is why hams call it the 2 meter band. Now try the 20 meter band. It uses about 14 megahertz (MHz). You divide 300 by 14, and you get about 21. So the waves are about 21 meters long. The lower frequency has the longer wave. The rule works every time.

What to remember

  • Frequency is how many times a wave repeats in one second.
  • Wavelength is the distance from one peak to the next peak.
  • When frequency goes up, wavelength gets shorter.
  • A radio wave moves at the speed of light.
  • A radio wave has an electric field and a magnetic field, at right angles.
  • Divide 300 by the frequency in megahertz to find the wavelength in meters.
  • Bands are named by their wavelength.

  • Kilohertz (kHz): one thousand hertz.

Lesson 8: How radio waves travel

Welcome back. Lesson 7 was about frequency and wavelength. A radio wave moves at the speed of light, and its frequency sets its wavelength. Now let's see how a wave travels from one radio to another.

What you will learn

  • Three ways a wave travels from one radio to another.
  • Why some waves go far and some stay near.
  • What the ionosphere does.
  • Why a high antenna reaches farther.
  • Why HF reaches far and VHF and UHF stay near.

The idea in plain words

Radio waves travel in three main ways. The first way is a ground wave, which is a wave that follows the surface of the Earth. Ground waves work best at low frequencies, and they can travel for tens of miles.

The second way is line of sight, a straight path from one antenna to another. The wave goes in a straight line, so hills and buildings can block it. A higher antenna reaches farther. The air bends radio waves a small amount. To refract means to bend a wave. Your signal is the radio wave that your radio sends out. So your signal can reach a bit past the horizon. A repeater sits on a high place for this reason. A repeater is a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. Its high spot gives it a long line of sight.

A wave can bounce off buildings and hills. Then it may reach your antenna by several paths. This is multipath propagation. The waves can cancel or reinforce each other. So strength can vary a lot in a few feet. In a moving car this fast flutter is called picket fencing. Multipath also hurts data. It makes error rates go up.

Long-distance CW and SSB contacts on VHF and UHF use horizontal polarization. The other station may use the opposite polarization. Then your signal gets weaker. This is cross-polarization. Vegetation is trees and plants. It absorbs UHF and microwave signals. So weak signals sound poor. Precipitation is rain and snow. It can cut range at microwave frequencies. Buildings can block your path to a repeater. Find a path that reflects your signal to it. Signals can bend over obstructions, past the line of sight. This is knife-edge diffraction.

The third way is a sky wave. High above the Earth there is the ionosphere. The ionosphere is layers of charged air high above the Earth that can reflect radio waves. A sky wave is a wave that bounces off the ionosphere and comes back down far away. A sky wave can travel hundreds or thousands of miles. It can reach a person on the other side of the world.

Hams split radio frequencies into three groups. HF is high frequency, from 3 to 30 megahertz. VHF is very high frequency, from 30 to 300 megahertz. UHF is ultra high frequency, from 300 to 3000 megahertz. HF signals often travel by sky wave, and that is why HF bands reach so far. VHF and UHF signals mostly travel by line of sight, because the ionosphere does not often reflect them. That is why VHF and UHF bands stay near.

An example

You live in a town, and your friend lives 10 miles away. You both have radios for the 2 meter band, so you call your friend on 146.52 megahertz (MHz). Your signal travels in a straight line, from your antenna to your friend's antenna. But a hill stands between you, and the hill blocks your signal. Your friend hears nothing. So you raise your antenna higher, and now your signal clears the hill. Your friend hears you well.

Later you try the 20 meter band, and you call on about 14 megahertz (MHz). Your signal goes up to the ionosphere, bounces off, and comes down far away. A person 500 miles away answers your call. Your wave traveled by sky wave. The band and the path set how far it goes.

What to remember

  • A ground wave follows the surface of the Earth.
  • Line of sight is a straight path from one antenna to another.
  • A sky wave bounces off the ionosphere and comes back down far away.
  • HF often uses sky wave and reaches far.
  • VHF and UHF mostly use line of sight and stay near.
  • The air refracts radio waves, so they reach a bit past the horizon.
  • Multipath makes signal strength vary a lot in a few feet.

Words to know

  • Antenna: the part of your station that sends and receives radio waves.
  • Ground wave: a wave that follows the surface of the Earth.
  • HF (high frequency): radio frequencies from 3 to 30 megahertz.
  • Ionosphere: layers of charged air high above the Earth that can reflect radio waves.
  • Knife-edge diffraction: signals bend over obstructions.
  • Line of sight: a straight path from one antenna to another.
  • Multipath propagation: a signal that takes several paths.
  • Picket fencing: fast flutter on a mobile signal.
  • Refract: to bend a wave.
  • Repeater: a station on a high place that hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger.
  • Signal: the radio wave that your radio sends out.
  • Sky wave: a wave that bounces off the ionosphere and comes back down far away.
  • UHF (ultra high frequency): radio frequencies from 300 to 3000 megahertz.
  • VHF (very high frequency): radio frequencies from 30 to 300 megahertz.

Lesson 9: The ionosphere: day, night, and sunspots

Welcome back. Lessons 7 and 8 covered radio waves. Now let's see why the same band can reach far away by day and go quiet at night. It's all about the sun.

What you will learn

  • What the ionosphere is.
  • How the sun builds the ionosphere each day.
  • Why one band changes from day to night.
  • What sunspots are and what they do.
  • Why the 10 meter band opens in some years and not in others.
  • When to try to reach far places on 10 meters.

The idea in plain words

The ionosphere is layers of charged air high above the Earth that can reflect radio waves. It has three main layers, and hams call them the D layer, the E layer, and the F layer. The pool questions call each one a region, and layer and region mean the same thing here. The D layer is the lowest, and the F layer is the highest. The F layer does most of the work for long-distance contacts. A contact is a two-way talk by radio.

The sun makes the ionosphere. Sunlight hits the air high above the Earth and changes it, and the changed air can reflect radio waves. To reflect means to bounce a wave back. Each morning the sun builds the layers up again, and each night they fade. This daily change is why bands open and close.

By day the D layer is strong, and it absorbs low HF signals. HF is high frequency, from 3 to 30 megahertz. To absorb means to take in and weaken a wave. The 80 meter and 40 meter bands are low bands, and the 40 meter band uses about 7 megahertz (MHz). A signal is the radio wave that your radio sends out. By day its signals do not reach the F layer, so they cannot bounce back from far away. That is why the low bands only reach a short way by day.

At night the sun is gone, and the D layer fades away. Low band signals now reach the F layer, and the F layer bounces them back far away. At night the low bands open for long-distance contacts. The same band that stays near by day can reach across the country at night.

The F layer stays at night but grows weak. It still reflects low band signals, but it cannot reflect high band signals well. So the high bands close at night. The 20 meter band uses about 14 megahertz (MHz), and it is open by day and quiet at night.

Sunspots change the ionosphere too. A sunspot is a dark spot on the sun, and more sunspots mean more energy from the sun. More energy makes a stronger ionosphere, and a stronger ionosphere reflects higher frequencies. Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. Sunspots rise and fall in a cycle. This rise and fall is the sunspot cycle. One cycle lasts about 11 years. The peak is the high point of the cycle. At the peak, sunspots are many. At the low point, sunspots are few.

At the peak of the sunspot cycle the 10 meter and 6 meter bands open for long distance. They use the F region of the ionosphere. The 10 meter band uses about 28 megahertz (MHz). The 6 meter band runs from 50 to 54 megahertz (MHz). The best time is from dawn to shortly after sunset, when the sun is up and strong. An opening is a time when a band reaches far, and high sunspot activity makes the opening last longer. At night these bands close, and in low sunspot years they may stay closed for months.

F1 and F2 are the two parts of the F layer. The sun sits at the top left, with rays that shine down on the layers. An antenna is the part of your station that sends and receives radio waves. A line rises from an antenna on the Earth. It hits the F layer and bounces back down, landing far from where it started. A sky wave is a wave that bounces off the ionosphere and comes back down far away. The drawing shows a sky wave at work. You do not need the picture to learn this fact.

An example

You try the 10 meter band at noon, when sunspots are near their peak. You call on about 28 megahertz (MHz), and your signal goes up to the F layer. It bounces back down far away, and a person in another country answers you. Then you try the same band at midnight, and you hear nothing. The F layer is too weak at night for 10 meters, so the band is closed. You switch to the 40 meter band, and now you hear people from far away. The D layer is gone, and the low band is open. The same sky acts in a different way by day and by night.

Lesson 10: Beyond the horizon

Welcome back. Lesson 9 was about the ionosphere: day, night, and sunspots. The sun builds the ionosphere each day. The F layer bounces HF signals back for long-distance contacts. Now let's look past the horizon. Some signals reach far past it in ways you may not expect.

What you will learn

  • What sporadic E is and which bands it helps.
  • What tropospheric ducting is and what causes it.
  • What auroral backscatter sounds like.
  • Which band is best for meteor scatter.
  • Why some signals fade in an odd way.
  • What fog and rain do to 10-meter and 6-meter signals.
  • Why either antenna works for bounced signals.

The idea in plain words

Sometimes the E layer grows strong in small spots. This is sporadic E. The word sporadic means now and then, not all the time. Sporadic E can bounce signals back from far past the horizon. It gives strong signals on the 10, 6, and 2 meter bands. These signals come from beyond the radio horizon. They do not last long. But they can be very strong while they last.

The troposphere is the air near the ground, below the ionosphere. At times a layer of warm air sits over cool air. This is a temperature inversion. Warm air over cool air can trap VHF and UHF signals in a duct. A duct is like a pipe in the sky. Signals in the duct can travel past the horizon, to about 300 miles. This happens on a regular basis. Hams call it tropospheric ducting.

Sometimes the sky glows near the poles. This glow is the aurora. The aurora can bounce VHF signals back to Earth. Hams call this auroral backscatter. The signals sound odd. They are distorted, with a raspy sound. Raspy means rough and harsh.

Small bits of rock fall into the air each day. We call them meteors. A meteor burns as it falls, and it leaves a short trail of charged air. A VHF signal can bounce off that trail. Hams call this meteor scatter. The best band for meteor scatter is 6 meters.

Signals that bounce off the ionosphere can fade in an odd way. The fade comes and goes with no clear pattern. This is irregular fading. It happens when parts of one signal take different paths. The parts meet at your antenna at slightly different times. They add up and cancel out at random. This random combining makes the signal grow strong, then weak, then strong again.

Fog and rain do little to 10-meter and 6-meter signals. They do not soak up the signals. They do not bend them away. You can still talk on 10 meters and 6 meters in fog and rain.

Polarization is the way a radio wave lines up as it travels. A wave from a vertical antenna is vertical. A wave from a horizontal antenna is horizontal. But signals that bounce off the ionosphere come back elliptically polarized. The wave twists as it travels. So it is part vertical and part horizontal at once. That is good news. Either a vertical or a horizontal antenna may be used to send or receive these signals. You do not need to match it.

An example

You turn on your radio on a warm summer night. You tune to 6 meters, and you hear a station 500 miles away. Your antenna is low, and the station is far past the horizon. But the signal is strong. A patch of sporadic E is bouncing your signal back. An hour later the station is gone. Sporadic E comes and goes. That is why hams call it sporadic.

What to remember

  • Sporadic E gives strong signals past the horizon on 10, 6, and 2 meters.
  • Tropospheric ducting carries VHF and UHF to about 300 miles on a regular basis.
  • Temperature inversions in the atmosphere cause tropospheric ducting.
  • Auroral backscatter sounds distorted and raspy.
  • 6 meters is the best band for meteor scatter.
  • Irregular fading comes from signals that take different paths and combine at random.
  • Fog and rain have little effect on 10-meter and 6-meter signals.
  • Ionosphere signals are elliptically polarized, so either antenna works.

Words to know

  • Aurora: a glow in the sky near the poles. It can bounce VHF signals back to Earth.
  • Auroral backscatter: VHF signals bounced back by the aurora. They sound distorted and raspy.
  • Duct: a layer in the air that traps signals and guides them far.
  • Elliptically polarized: twisted so the wave is part vertical and part horizontal at once.
  • Irregular fading: a signal that grows strong and weak with no clear pattern.
  • Meteor scatter: signals bounced off the trail of a falling meteor.
  • Polarization: the way a radio wave lines up as it travels.
  • Raspy: rough and harsh in sound.
  • Sporadic E: strong spots in the E layer that bounce signals back now and then.
  • Temperature inversion: warm air sitting over cool air.
  • Troposphere: the air near the ground, below the ionosphere.
  • Tropospheric ducting: VHF and UHF signals trapped in a duct, traveling past the horizon.

Lesson 13: Electricity: volts, amps, and ohms

Last time you set up a station. Now let's look at the three ideas that run all of it.

What you will learn

  • What voltage is, and what the volt measures.
  • What current is, and what the amp measures.
  • What resistance is, and what the ohm measures.
  • Ohm's law in plain words.
  • How to work out each one with a short sum.

The idea in plain words

Your radio runs on electric power. Three ideas rule how that power acts: voltage, current, and resistance. You'll need all three to grasp Ohm's law, so let's take them one at a time.

Voltage is the push that moves electric current through a wire. The unit for voltage is the volt, and we write volts as V. A flashlight battery gives 1.5 volts, and a car battery gives 12 volts. More volts mean a stronger push.

Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. Electrons are tiny bits inside matter. The unit for current is the ampere. Hams say amp for short, and we write amps as A. A small radio may draw 1 amp, while a big radio may draw 20 amps. More amps mean more flow.

Milli means one thousandth. One milliampere is one thousandth of an ampere. So 1.5 amperes is 1500 milliamperes. And 3000 milliamperes is 3 amperes. Micro means one millionth. One microvolt is one one-millionth of a volt.

Resistance is how much a part fights the flow of current. The unit for resistance is the ohm, and we write ohms with the sign Ω. A long thin wire has more resistance than a short thick wire. A resistor is a part that limits the flow of current. It adds resistance on purpose.

Ohm's law links the three ideas in one rule. It says voltage equals current times resistance. We write the rule with three short letters. E stands for voltage, I stands for current, and R stands for resistance. The short form is E = I x RE equals I times R.

You can turn the rule around to find each one. To find current, divide volts by ohms. We write that as I = E / RI equals E divided by R. To find voltage, multiply amps by ohms. We write that as E = I x RE equals I times R. To find resistance, divide volts by amps. We write that as R = E / IR equals E divided by I.

The picture with this lesson shows the Ohm's law triangle. The triangle has three parts. E sits at the top, I sits at the bottom left, and R sits at the bottom right. A line splits the top part from the bottom part, and that line means divide. There's no line between I and R, and that means multiply. Cover the letter you want to find, and the rest shows the sum you must do. To find I, cover I. You see E over R, and that means E divided by R. To find E, cover E. You see I next to R, and that means I times R. To find R, cover R. You see E over I, and that means E divided by I.

You don't need to learn the triangle by heart. The three short forms say the same thing, so use the form that fits the sum in front of you.

Why does this count for hams? Your radio draws current from its power source, and current flows in a circuit. A circuit is a path that current flows around. A fuse is a part that breaks the circuit if too much current flows. If you know volts and amps, you can pick the right fuse. You can also pick wire thick enough for the current. Thin wire with too much current gets hot, and hot wire can start a fire.

A multimeter is a meter that measures voltage, current, and resistance. Hams use a multimeter to check a circuit, and you'll meet the multimeter again in Lesson 20.

An example

Let's work through all three forms. A radio draws 2 amps from a 12-volt source. What is the resistance of the circuit?

You know volts and amps, and you want ohms, so use R = E / IR equals E divided by I. Put 12 in place of E and 2 in place of I, then divide 12 by 2. The answer is 6 ohms.

Now let's find current. A lamp has 6 ohms of resistance and runs on 12 volts. How much current flows? Use I = E / RI equals E divided by R. Put 12 in place of E and 6 in place of R, then divide 12 by 6. The answer is 2 amps.

And now voltage. A lamp draws 2 amps and has 6 ohms of resistance. How many volts does it need? Use E = I x RE equals I times R. Put 2 in place of I and 6 in place of R, then multiply 2 by 6. The answer is 12 volts.

Check your sum each time. A wrong step gives a wrong answer. The exam asks for one of the three forms, so know which form fits the facts you have.

  • Voltage is the push. Its unit is the volt.
  • Current is the flow. Its unit is the amp.
  • Resistance fights the flow. Its unit is the ohm.
  • Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance.
  • I = E / RI equals E divided by R finds current.
  • E = I x RE equals I times R finds voltage.
  • R = E / IR equals E divided by I finds resistance.
  • Cover the letter you want in the triangle. The rest shows the sum to do.
  • Milli means one thousandth. Micro means one millionth.

Words to know

Lesson 14: Power and the decibel

Last time you met volts, amps, and ohms. Now let's talk about power: how much your radio uses, and how hams compare power levels.

What you will learn

  • What power is, and what the watt measures.
  • How to work out power from volts and amps.
  • What a decibel is, and how it compares two power levels.
  • What gain and loss mean in decibels.

The idea in plain words

Power is the rate at which electric energy is used. The unit for power is the watt, and we write watts as W. A 100-watt radio uses 100 watts when it sends, while a small lamp may use 5 watts. More watts mean more power.

Milli means one thousandth. So 500 milliwatts is 0.5 watts.

You can work out power from volts and amps. Power equals voltage times current. Voltage is the push that moves electric current through a wire. Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. Electrons are tiny bits inside matter. The unit for voltage is the volt, and the unit for current is the ampere. Hams say amp for short. We write the rule as P = E x IP equals E times I. P stands for power, E stands for voltage, and I stands for current.

Watts and volts are not the same thing. Volts tell you the push, and watts tell you the rate of use. A 12-volt radio can use 5 watts or 100 watts. The volts stay the same, and the watts change with the current.

Hams care about power for two reasons. First, power sets how strong your signal is. Your signal is the radio wave that carries your voice, and more watts can reach farther. Second, power sets what your radio needs from its power source. A 100-watt radio needs more current than a 5-watt radio.

The decibel is a unit that compares two power levels. We write decibel as dB. A decibel does not tell you how many watts you have. It tells you how one power level stands next to another. It is a ratio, and a ratio is one number set next to another.

Hams use decibels to talk about gain and loss. Gain is how much stronger a signal gets, and loss is how much weaker a signal gets. A gain of 3 decibels means the power doubles, and a loss of 3 decibels means the power halves. In an amplifier, gain is the output signal set next to the input signal. It can be a voltage, current, or power ratio.

Two facts will carry you far. A change of 3 dB means double or half. A change of 10 dB means 10 times or one tenth. So 5 watts plus 3 dB is 10 watts, and 10 watts plus 10 dB is 100 watts. 10 watts minus 3 dB is 5 watts.

Decibels add up in a plain way. Two gains of 3 dB make 6 dB, and two doubles make four times the power. So a 6 dB gain means four times the power. A 10 dB gain plus a 3 dB gain is 13 dB, and that means 20 times the power.

0 dB means no change. The power stays the same. A minus sign means loss, and a loss of 10 dB leaves one tenth of the power.

Feed lines lose power too. A feed line is the cable that carries radio signals between the radio and the antenna. An antenna is the part of a station that sends and picks up radio waves. A long feed line may lose 3 dB. That means half your power turns to heat before it reaches the antenna. Short feed lines lose less.

You'll see dB on antennas too. An antenna with 3 dB of gain sends twice the power in its best direction. It does not make new power. It aims the power it has.

An example

A radio runs on 12 volts and draws 5 amps. How much power does it use? Use P = E x IP equals E times I. Put 12 in place of E and 5 in place of I, then multiply 12 by 5. The answer is 60 watts.

Now try a decibel sum. A signal is 5 watts, and it gains 3 dB. How strong is it now? A gain of 3 dB doubles the power, so double 5 watts. The answer is 10 watts.

Try one more. A signal is 100 watts, and it loses 10 dB. How strong is it now? A loss of 10 dB leaves one tenth, and one tenth of 100 is 10. The answer is 10 watts.

Try a gain of 10 dB. A signal is 5 watts, and it gains 10 dB. How strong is it now? A gain of 10 dB means 10 times, and 10 times 5 is 50. The answer is 50 watts.

Note how small the dB numbers stay. A jump from 5 watts to 500 watts is 20 dB. Big power jumps need only small dB numbers, and that is why hams use decibels.

  • Power is the rate at which electric energy is used.
  • The watt is the unit for power.
  • P = E x IP equals E times I finds power.
  • A decibel compares two power levels.
  • A gain of 3 dB doubles power.
  • A gain of 10 dB means 10 times the power.
  • A loss of 3 dB halves power.
  • 0 dB means no change.

  • watt: the unit for power.
  • milli: one thousandth.
  • milliwatt: one thousandth of a watt.
  • volt: the unit for voltage.
  • voltage: the push that moves electric current through a wire.
  • ampere (amp): the unit for current.
  • current: the flow of electrons through a wire.
  • electron: a tiny bit inside matter. Its flow through a wire is current.

Lesson 15: Frequency, wavelength, and alternating current

Last time you worked with power and decibels. Now let's look at the current itself, and how its speed sets the size of your antenna.

What you will learn

  • What alternating current is, and how it differs from direct current.
  • What a sine wave is, the shape of smooth AC.
  • How frequency and wavelength link together.
  • How to change one into the other with a short sum.

The idea in plain words

Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. Electrons are tiny bits inside matter. Direct current (DC) flows in one direction only. A battery gives DC. Alternating current (AC) changes direction many times each second, and house power is AC. A power supply is a box that changes house power into the DC power a radio needs.

The picture with this lesson shows a sine wave, which is the shape of smooth AC. The picture has a flat line across the middle, and that line stands for zero. The curve rises above the line, then falls back to it. It dips below the line, then rises back to it. One full rise and fall is one cycle.

The top of the curve is the peak, the strongest point. The curve spends half its time above the line and half below it. The two halves match in size and shape.

Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. The unit for frequency is the hertz, and one hertz is one repeat per second. We write hertz as Hz. House power repeats 60 times each second, so we say it is 60 hertz (Hz). A radio wave can repeat millions of times each second, and we count those in megahertz (MHz). One megahertz is one million hertz.

Hams also use kilohertz (kHz). One kilohertz is one thousand hertz, so a wave on 3,500 kilohertz is the same as 3.5 megahertz. To change kilohertz to megahertz, divide by 1,000. Hams also use gigahertz (GHz). One gigahertz is 1000 megahertz. So 2425 MHz is 2.425 GHz.

Wavelength is the distance from one peak of a wave to the next peak. The second picture shows this. It shows a wave with peaks and dips. An arrow marks the gap from one peak to the next peak. That gap is one wavelength.

Frequency and wavelength link in a fixed way. As one goes up, the other goes down. A high frequency means a short wavelength, and a low frequency means a long wavelength. The link is a simple sum. Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz.

The number 300 comes from the speed of light. Radio waves move at the speed of light, which is about 300 million meters each second. The sum uses 300 as a short form of that speed.

A radio wave at 146.52 megahertz (MHz) has a wavelength near 2 meters, and hams call this the 2-meter band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. A radio wave at 14 MHz has a wavelength near 21 meters, and hams call this the 20-meter band. A radio wave at 440 MHz has a wavelength near 70 centimeters, and hams call this the 70-centimeter band.

An antenna is the part of a station that sends and picks up radio waves, and wavelength sets antenna size. A dipole is a simple antenna made of two straight wires, and its length is about half a wavelength. A 2-meter wave needs an antenna about 1 meter long, and a 20-meter wave needs one about 10 meters long. That is why low bands need big antennas.

The current in your antenna is AC. It changes direction at the frequency of the wave. At 146.52 MHz, it changes direction 146.52 million times each second. That fast change is what makes a radio wave.

An example

What is the wavelength of a wave at 146.52 MHz? Use the sum: divide 300 by 146.52. The answer is near 2 meters.

What is the wavelength of a wave at 28 MHz? Divide 300 by 28. The answer is near 10.7 meters, and hams call this the 10-meter band.

You can also go the other way. What is the frequency of a wave 40 meters long? Divide 300 by 40. The answer is 7.5 MHz.

What is the frequency of a 2-meter wave? Divide 300 by 2. The answer is 150 MHz, and the 2-meter band sits near 150 MHz.

A shortcut helps you check your work. If the frequency is high, the wavelength must be short. So if your sum gives a long wavelength for a high frequency, check the sum again.

What to remember

  • DC flows in one direction only.
  • AC changes direction many times each second.
  • One full rise and fall is one cycle.
  • Frequency counts repeats each second.
  • Wavelength is peak to peak.
  • Wavelength in meters equals 300 divided by frequency in megahertz.
  • High frequency means short wavelength.
  • Low frequency means long wavelength.
  • Wavelength sets antenna size.
  • To change kilohertz to megahertz, divide by 1,000.

  • electron: a tiny bit inside matter. Its flow through a wire is current.
  • direct current (DC): current that flows in one direction only.
  • alternating current (AC): current that changes direction many times each second.
  • power supply: a box that changes house power into the DC power a radio needs.
  • sine wave: the shape of smooth AC.
  • cycle: one full rise and fall of a wave.
  • frequency: how many times a radio wave repeats in one second.
  • hertz (Hz): the unit for frequency. One hertz is one repeat per second.
  • kilohertz (kHz): one thousand hertz.
  • megahertz (MHz): one million hertz.
  • gigahertz (GHz): 1000 megahertz.
  • wavelength: the distance from one peak of a wave to the next peak.
  • band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.

  • dipole: a simple antenna made of two straight wires.

Lesson 16: Resistors, capacitors, and coils

Last time you met frequency, wavelength, and alternating current. Now let's open up the radio. Meet three parts you'll find in almost every one.

What you will learn

A circuit is a path that current follows through the parts in a radio. Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. This lesson covers three parts you'll find in almost every radio.

  • What a resistor does in a circuit.
  • What a capacitor does in a circuit.
  • What a coil does in a circuit.
  • How to tell the three parts apart by their jobs.

The idea in plain words

A radio holds many small parts. Each part has one job. Hams also call these parts components. Three parts show up in almost every radio. They are the resistor, the capacitor, and the coil. You can tell them apart by what each one does.

A resistor is a part that limits the flow of current. It opposes the flow. Resistance is how much a part fights the flow of current. The unit for resistance is the ohm. A resistor with more ohms fights current more. A resistor turns some energy into heat. A big resistor can handle more heat than a small one. You use a resistor to set how much current flows in one part of a circuit. A resistor is often a small tube. Colored bands go around the tube. The bands tell you how many ohms the resistor has.

Direct current (DC) flows in one direction only. Alternating current (AC) changes direction many times each second. House power is AC. A resistor works the same with DC and with AC. It fights both kinds of current the same way.

A capacitor is a part that stores energy in an electric field. An electric field is the space around a part. That is where it stores energy. A capacitor takes in energy and holds it. Then it gives the energy back. A capacitor has two flat plates with a small gap between them. The plates are conductive surfaces. They carry current. The gap is an insulator. It blocks current. The energy sits in the gap. A big capacitor holds more energy than a small one. A capacitor stops DC from passing through it. It lets AC pass. Hams use capacitors to store energy for a short time. They also use them to block DC where it's not wanted. Capacitance is the ability to store energy in an electric field. The unit of capacitance is the farad.

Micro means one millionth. Pico means one million millionth. So 1,000,000 picofarads is 1 microfarad.

A coil is also called an inductor. An inductor is a part that stores energy in a magnetic field. It's often a coil of wire. A magnetic field is the space around a coil. That is where it stores energy. A coil is made of wire wound in loops. A coil with more loops stores more energy. A coil with few loops stores less. Some coils have an iron core inside. The iron makes the magnetic field stronger. A coil fights changes in current. A coil lets DC pass. It fights AC. A coil and a capacitor act in opposite ways. Inductance is the ability to store energy in a magnetic field. The unit of inductance is the henry.

You can test a part by what it does. Put DC through it. A resistor lets some current through. A capacitor stops the current. A coil lets the current through. Now use AC. A resistor acts the same. A capacitor lets the current through. A coil fights the current. These tests tell you which part you have.

In series, the current is the same through all parts. In parallel, the voltage is the same across all parts.

A capacitor with an inductor makes a resonant circuit. It is also called a tuned circuit. It is an inductor and a capacitor in series or parallel.

Some batteries can be charged again. Nickel-metal hydride, lithium-ion, and lead-acid batteries are all rechargeable. Carbon-zinc batteries are not rechargeable.

An example

You press the power switch on your radio. A small light comes on. A resistor sits in the wire to the light. It limits the current. The light gets just enough to shine. It does not burn out. The resistor keeps the light safe. Inside the radio, a capacitor stores energy. It gives the energy back when the radio needs it fast. A coil sits in another part of the radio. It stores energy in a magnetic field. It fights fast changes in current. The capacitor and the coil keep the current steady. Each part does its own job. The radio needs all three parts to work well.

  • Inductance stores energy in a magnetic field. Its unit is the henry.
  • In series, the current is the same through all parts. In parallel, the voltage is the same across all parts.
  • A resonant circuit is a coil and a capacitor in series or parallel.
  • 1,000,000 picofarads is 1 microfarad.

The idea in plain words

A radio holds many small parts. Some parts are made from semiconductors. Most are made of silicon, a material found in sand. A semiconductor can let current flow. It can also stop current. This control makes diodes and transistors work.

Some materials let current flow with ease. They are called conductors. Some materials stop current. They are called insulators. A semiconductor sits between the two. It can act like a conductor, and it can also act like an insulator. Metals are good conductors. They have many free electrons. A free electron is an electron that can move with ease. Glass is a good insulator. Plastic and rubber are good insulators too.

A diode is a part that lets current flow in one direction only. Current can go through it one way, but it cannot go back the other way. A diode has two wires. Current enters through one wire. It leaves through the other. If you put a diode in the wrong way, no current flows. Alternating current (AC) changes direction many times each second. Direct current (DC) flows in one direction only. Hams use diodes to turn AC into DC. A diode lets one half of the AC through and blocks the other half. A circuit that turns AC into DC is called a rectifier. Each diode has a forward voltage drop. That is a small amount of voltage the diode uses up. The drop is lower in some diode types than in others. The two wires of a diode are called the anode and the cathode. The cathode lead is often marked with a stripe on the package.

One kind of diode gives off light. It is called a light emitting diode (LED). An LED gives off light when current flows through it. Hams use LEDs as small lights in radios.

A transistor is a part that can switch current on and off. It can also make a signal stronger. A signal is an electric current that carries sound or data. A transistor has three wires, called the base, the collector, and the emitter. A transistor has three regions of semiconductor material inside it. The base is the control wire. A small current at the base turns the transistor on. No current at the base turns it off. A small current at the base controls a big current through it. This is how it switches current on and off. This is why a small part can do a big job. A transistor can also make a weak signal stronger. Hams use them in almost every radio. One kind of transistor is the field-effect transistor. FET stands for Field Effect Transistor. A field-effect transistor has a gate, a drain, and a source.

A chip can hold a whole radio circuit. A chip saves space in a radio. Your radio would be far bigger without chips. Hams use ICs to do many jobs at once.

A relay is an electrically-controlled switch. A small current turns the relay on and off. The relay then opens or closes a bigger circuit.

A diode controls which way current flows. A transistor controls how much current flows. An IC packs many parts into one chip. These three parts work together in your radio.

How do you tell them apart? A diode has two wires, and a transistor has three wires. An IC is a small flat box with many short legs. Each leg is a wire. You can feel the legs. Touch tells you a lot. You can count them, and the count tells you what the part is.

An example

One small chip in the radio holds thousands of parts. That chip is an IC. It does many jobs at once, and it saves space. All three parts work at once when you use the radio.

Lesson 19: Receivers and transmitters

Last time you learned the three exam diagrams by their part numbers. Now let's look at what your radio does with a signal: how it hears one, and how it sends one.

What you will learn

  • How your radio hears a weak signal.
  • How your radio sends your voice out.
  • How one box can both hear and send.
  • How one switch changes your radio from hear to send.

The idea in plain words

A radio has two main jobs. It hears signals, and it sends signals. A signal is a radio wave that carries a voice or a message. The part that hears is the receiver. The part that sends is the transmitter. Most radios hold both parts in one box. That box is a transceiver. A transceiver is one box. It holds a receiver and a transmitter.

The receiver works in four steps. First, the antenna catches signals from the air. An antenna is the part that sends and catches radio waves. The signals are weak because they have traveled a long way. Second, you tune the radio to pick one signal. To tune means to set the radio to one frequency. Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. You enter the frequency with the keypad or the VFO knob. The VFO circuit sets the receive and transmit frequency. VFO stands for Variable Frequency Oscillator. The receiver drops all the other signals. Sensitivity is the ability of a receiver to detect the presence of a signal. Selectivity is the ability of a receiver to discriminate between multiple signals. Third, an amplifier makes the signal stronger. An amplifier is a part that makes a signal stronger. Fourth, the receiver turns the signal into sound. You hear a voice from the speaker. A speaker is the part that makes sound you can hear.

A mixer converts a signal from one frequency to another. An oscillator is a circuit that generates a signal at a specific frequency. Some radios let you pick the receiver filter bandwidth. A filter bandwidth is how wide a slice of signal the receiver lets through. Pick a bandwidth that matches the mode. This cuts noise and interference.

The transmitter works in four steps too. First, you talk into the microphone. A microphone is the part that hears your voice. It turns your voice into a signal the radio can use. Second, the transmitter turns your voice into a radio signal. This step is called modulation. Modulation means changing a signal so it can carry a voice. Third, an amplifier makes the radio signal strong. Fourth, the signal goes to the antenna. The antenna sends it out into the air.

You switch from hear to send with one switch. The switch is called PTT. PTT stands for push to talk. On most radios, the switch sits on the microphone. You press it to talk. You let it go to listen. The PTT input is the place where the switch joins the radio. When you press the switch, the PTT input joins to ground. A ground is a safe path. It lets electric current flow into the Earth. Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. To ground a wire means to join it to ground. When the PTT input is grounded, the transceiver switches from receive to transmit.

A transceiver hears or sends, but not both at the same time. One ham talks while the other listens. Then they swap. This is how most ham talks work.

A transverter converts the RF input and output of a transceiver to another band. Some VHF power amplifiers have a switch. The switch selects SSB or CW-FM. It sets the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode.

The third box makes the signal stronger. The fourth box takes out the voice. The last box is the speaker. Arrows show the signal moving from box to box. The bottom row is the transmitter. It has four boxes in a line. The first box is the microphone. The second box puts your voice on the signal. The third box makes the signal strong. The last box is the antenna. Arrows show the voice moving from box to box. You don't need the picture to learn this lesson. The words here tell you all of it.

An example

Sam wants to talk to a friend. He turns his radio on. His radio is a transceiver. It hears and sends in one box. Sam tunes to his friend's frequency. He hears his friend call. The receiver catches a weak signal. It makes the signal strong and turns it into sound. Sam presses the PTT switch, and the radio switches from hear to send. He talks into the microphone. The transmitter turns his voice into a radio signal. It makes the signal strong. The antenna sends it out. Sam lets go of the switch. The radio switches back to hear. His friend answers.

Lesson 20: Power supplies and meters

What you will learn

The idea in plain words

Your radio needs the right power to work. Power is the rate at which electric energy is used. A watt is the unit for power. Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. An ampere is the unit for current. House power is alternating current, or AC. That means it changes direction many times each second. Your radio needs direct current, or DC. DC flows in one direction only. A power supply is the box that changes house power. It makes the DC power your radio needs. Most radios run on 13.8 volts. Voltage is the push that moves electric current through a wire. A volt is the unit for voltage.

There are two kinds of power supply. Each has a good side and a bad side. A linear power supply uses a transformer to change the voltage. A transformer is a part that changes AC voltage. It changes it from one level to another. A linear supply is heavy. But it makes very little noise. Noise is hiss and crackle that hides weak signals. A switching power supply turns the power on and off very fast. That changes the voltage. A switching supply is light. But it can make noise that you hear in your radio. Whichever kind you pick, make sure it gives enough current. A 50-watt mobile radio needs 12 amperes. So your supply must give 12 amperes or more. A regulator controls the amount of voltage from a power supply. It keeps the voltage at the right level.

You check your radio with a meter. A multimeter is a meter that measures voltage, current, and resistance. Resistance is how much a part fights the flow of current. An ohm is the unit for resistance. The meter has a switch. You turn it to pick what you want to check. Set it to volts for voltage. Set it to amps for current. Set it to ohms for resistance. The meter has two leads. A lead is a wire with a metal tip. You touch the tips to the points you want to check. The red lead goes to the plus side. The black lead goes to the minus side. How you join the meter matters. A voltmeter measures applied voltage. Connect it in parallel with a component. Parallel means the meter joins across the part. To measure current, connect the meter in series with a component. Series means the current flows through the meter.

Each job has its own meter. Your multimeter holds all three in one box. A voltmeter measures voltage. Voltage is also called electric potential. So a voltmeter measures electric potential. An ammeter measures electric current. An ohmmeter sends a small current through the part. Then it measures the voltage that results. One rule matters here. Check resistance only when the power is off. A circuit is the path that current follows. A live circuit is a circuit with the power on. A live circuit can break the meter. So switch the power off first. Never try to measure voltage while the meter is set to resistance. That mistake can damage the multimeter.

A capacitor is a part that stores electric charge. Connect an ohmmeter across a large, discharged capacitor. The reading goes up with time. The resistance seems to increase. That is normal.

An example

Then he checks the fuse. A fuse is a part that breaks the circuit. It breaks if too much current flows. This one is fine. In the end Lee finds a loose wire. He tightens it, and the radio works again. Step by step, his meter helped him find the fault.

Soldering joins wires and parts with melted metal called solder. Use rosin-core solder for radio work. Never use acid-core solder for radio and electronic work. A good joint looks smooth and shiny. A cold tin-lead solder joint has a rough or lumpy surface.

Mia is glad. Her radio and antenna both work fine.

The idea in plain words

A mode is the way a radio sends a signal. Your voice can ride on a radio wave in more than one way. Each way is a mode. This lesson covers FM, the voice mode for local talk.

Modulation is how a radio puts your voice onto a radio wave. FM is short for frequency modulation. It changes the frequency of the signal to carry your voice. Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. The strength of the signal stays the same.

FM is the voice mode for very high frequency (VHF) and ultra high frequency (UHF) repeaters. VHF means radio frequencies from 30 to 300 megahertz. A megahertz (MHz) is one million hertz. UHF means radio frequencies from 300 to 3000 megahertz. A repeater is a station on a high place. It hears a weak signal and sends it out again, stronger. The bandwidth of an FM voice signal on a VHF repeater is between 10 and 15 kilohertz.

You use FM on the 2 meter band and the 70 centimeter band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. The 2 meter band is in VHF. The 70 centimeter band is in UHF. Most local ham talk happens on these two bands.

You can also use FM with no repeater at all. This is called simplex. Simplex means talking and listening on the same frequency. The national FM simplex calling frequency is 146.52 megahertz (MHz). Hams meet on that frequency and then move to another one to talk.

Most FM radios can scan. The scanning function tunes through a range of frequencies to check for activity. It stops when it hears a signal.

PM is short for phase modulation. PM is a voice mode much like FM. FM shifts the frequency with your voice, while PM shifts the timing of the wave instead. Most radios treat FM and PM the same. The exam groups them as one answer.

AM is short for amplitude modulation. AM is a voice mode that changes the strength of the signal. Hams rarely use AM for voice now. FM took its place for most local voice talk.

FM sounds clear and full. It sounds much like the FM radio in a car. Noise stays low on a good FM signal. But a weak FM signal can drop out all at once. One second you hear the voice, and the next second it is gone. FM does not change pitch. But if you tune an FM signal a little off, the sound gets distorted. FM has one drawback. Only one signal can be received at a time. This is the capture effect. The stronger FM signal captures the receiver. A weaker signal on the same frequency is not heard.

Talking too loudly causes a problem called over-deviation. Your FM audio can drop out on voice peaks. If someone says you are over-deviating, talk farther away from the microphone. That fixes it.

You may be speaking too loudly or too close to the microphone. Or you may be in a bad location. Any of these can cause the problem. A clip-on ferrite choke can fix distorted voice transmissions. It clips onto the microphone cable. It stops the transmitted signal from feeding back into the transmitter.

The idea in plain words

SSB is short for single sideband. SSB is a voice mode that sends only one side of the signal. A voice signal has three parts. The middle part is the carrier, the steady radio wave that carries your voice. The two side parts are sidebands. A sideband is one half of the voice signal, and it sits above or below the carrier. SSB sends only one sideband. It leaves out the carrier and the other sideband. SSB is a form of AM. AM is short for amplitude modulation. SSB comes from AM by leaving out the carrier and one sideband.

SSB is the main voice mode on high frequency (HF). HF means radio frequencies from 3 to 30 megahertz. Hams use SSB for long talks across the world. It takes less space in a band than FM. All of the power goes into the one sideband. A weak SSB signal fades bit by bit. But you can still hear words in a weak SSB signal.

Next to FM, SSB sounds thin. FM sounds full and rich. SSB sounds more like a voice on a phone line. If you tune a little off, SSB voices sound wrong. The pitch goes too high or too low. One rule matters a lot here: use the right sideband for the band you are on. Use upper sideband on 10 meter HF, VHF, and UHF. Upper sideband is the sideband above the carrier. Use lower sideband on HF bands below 10 megahertz. Lower sideband is the sideband below the carrier. If you use the wrong sideband, other hams cannot understand you.

Two settings shape how SSB sounds. Too much microphone gain causes distorted transmitted audio on SSB. Keep the gain low so your voice stays clean. A 2400 hertz receiver filter gives the best signal-to-noise ratio for SSB reception. It lets the voice through and cuts extra noise.

Lesson 24: Morse code and CW

The idea in plain words

Voice is not the only way to talk by radio. Hams also send code. This lesson covers Morse code and CW.

Morse code is a code of short and long signals that stand for letters. A short signal is called a dit. A long signal is called a dah. Each letter has its own mix of dits and dahs. The letter A is one dit and one dah. The letter B is one dah and three dits.

CW is Morse code sent by turning a signal on and off. You tap a key to send it, and a key is the switch you tap to send Morse code. An electronic keyer is a device that helps with manual sending of Morse code. Each tap turns the signal on for a short time. A short tap makes a dit, and a long tap makes a dah. The ham at the other end hears the taps as beeps, and the beeps spell out the letters.

CW takes very little space in a band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. CW takes less space than voice. A CW signal needs about 150 Hz of space. CW is narrower than FM voice, SSB voice, and slow-scan TV. CW also needs very little power. A weak CW signal can still get through when voice cannot. That is why hams still use CW for long talks. It works when the band is poor and the noise is high.

Two small parts of the VHF and UHF bands are for CW only. They are 50.0 to 50.1 MHz and 144.0 to 144.1 MHz.

Here is good news to know from the start: there is no Morse code test for any license. You do not need Morse code to get a license, not for Technician, General, or Extra. You can learn Morse code later if you want, but it is your choice, not a rule.

An example

You sit at your radio at night and tune to 40 meters. You hear short and long beeps. That is CW. Then you hear the letters C and Q. CQ means a ham wants to talk to any ham. So you tap back with your key and send your call sign in code. A call sign is the letters and numbers the FCC gives you as your name on the air. The other ham answers, and you talk in code for 10 minutes. The signal is weak, but each beep is clear.

  • A CW signal needs about 150 Hz, the narrowest of the common modes.

A digital mode is a mode that sends data, such as text, by radio. A mode is the way a radio sends a signal. Your radio links to a computer for most digital modes. You do not need to learn every mode at once. Start with one mode that fits your station. Try CW if you like code. Try FT8 if you like weak signals. Try packet radio if you like data.

Lesson 26: How antennas work

The last lesson covered digital modes.

Every radio needs an antenna to send your signal out and catch signals in. This lesson covers how an antenna works and how its length links to frequency.

What you will learn

  • What an antenna does.
  • How antenna length links to frequency.
  • What a dipole is and what it does well.
  • How to work out a dipole's length.

The idea in plain words

An antenna is a wire or rod that sends radio signals into the air. It also catches radio signals from the air. Your radio turns electric signals into radio waves. The antenna sends those waves out. It also catches waves and turns them back into electric signals.

Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. Wavelength is the distance from one peak of a wave to the next peak. A high frequency means a short wave, so it needs a short antenna. A low frequency means a long wave, so it needs a long antenna. An antenna must fit the wave it uses. An antenna is resonant when its length fits the wave it uses. It works best at that frequency.

Antenna loading makes an antenna act longer. It adds inductors to the radiating elements. This lets a short antenna work on a low band.

Polarization is the orientation of the electric field.

A dipole is a simple antenna made of two straight wires. The two wires sit in one straight line, and a feed line joins them in the middle. A feed line is the cable that carries radio signals between the radio and the antenna. The most common dipole is half a wave long. That means its full length is about half the wavelength. You can build one from plain wire and hang it between two trees.

The dipole sends its strongest signal broadside. Broadside means out to the sides, not off the ends. The strong signal goes out from the front and the back of the wire. Little signal goes out past the ends. So you aim a dipole by its sides, not by its ends.

The first picture shows a dipole. Two straight wires form one long line, and the feed line drops down from the center join. Arrows point out to the front and the back. They show where the signal is strongest. No arrows point past the ends.

Here is how you work out a dipole's length. Divide 468 by the frequency in megahertz. Megahertz (MHz) means one million hertz. Hertz (Hz) is the unit for frequency. One hertz is one repeat per second. The answer is the full length in feet. Each wire is half of that length. If you make the dipole shorter, its resonant frequency goes up.

An example

Say you want to use the 2 meter band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. The 2 meter band runs from 144 to 148 megahertz (MHz), and the national calling frequency is 146.52 megahertz (MHz). At that frequency, the wave is about 2 meters long. That is why hams call it the 2 meter band.

A half-wave dipole for 146.52 megahertz is short. Divide 468 by 146.52, and the answer is about 3.2 feet. Each wire is half of that, about 1.6 feet. Hang it high and clear of trees, and it will send its strongest signal out to the sides.

What to remember

  • An antenna sends signals out and catches signals in.
  • A dipole has two straight wires and sends strongest broadside.
  • A high frequency needs a short antenna, and a low frequency needs a long one.
  • A half-wave dipole is about 468 divided by the frequency in megahertz, in feet.
  • A resonant antenna fits the wave it uses.
  • Loading adds inductors to make an antenna act longer.

Words to know

Lesson 28: Feed lines and SWR

The last lesson covered antennas you will actually use. This one covers the cable that links your radio to your antenna. You'll learn what SWR means, why a good match matters, and what to do when the match is bad.

What you will learn

  • What a feed line does.
  • What coaxial cable is.
  • What standing wave ratio means.
  • What a good match looks like on a meter.
  • What an antenna tuner does.

The idea in plain words

A feed line is the cable that links your radio to your antenna. Your radio sends power up the feed line, and the antenna sends that power out as radio waves. The feed line also brings weak signals back down to your radio. So a bad feed line wastes power in both ways.

Coaxial cable, or coax, is the most common feed line. It has a center wire inside a shield. A shield is a metal layer around the center wire. The shield keeps noise out and keeps your signal in. The center wire carries the signal. A plastic layer sits between the wire and the shield, and an outer coat covers the whole cable. Coax is easy to use. You can run it through a wall, and you can bury some types in the ground.

Every feed line loses some signal. A long line loses more than a short line, and a high frequency loses more than a low frequency. So keep your feed line as short as you can, and use good cable for long runs. Water in the cable adds loss, so check the ends for damage. A loose connector adds loss too. Each connector adds a little loss. More connectors mean more loss.

Standing wave ratio (SWR) is a number that shows how well an antenna matches its feed line. Hams also call the feed line a transmission line, and the antenna is the load. A load is the part that uses the power. So SWR shows how well the load matches the line.

A match is about fit, and the fit is about impedance. Impedance is the resistance a part shows to radio signals. Most ham radios use 50 ohms. An ohm is the unit for resistance. The feed line is 50 ohms too, and the antenna should be 50 ohms as well. When all three are 50 ohms, the match is good. Power flows into the antenna, and little power bounces back.

When the match is bad, some power bounces back. The power that bounces back is called reflected power. Reflected power heats your radio. It does not reach the antenna, and it does not help your signal. A bad match wastes power and can harm your radio. Most solid-state radios cut power when SWR gets too high. They do this to protect the output transistors. That is why high SWR can mean low power out.

SWR is written as a ratio, like 1:1one to one. A reading of 1:1one to one means a perfect match, and no power bounces back. A reading of 1.5:1one point five to one is still good: about 4 percent of the power bounces back. A reading of 2:1two to one is fair, with about 11 percent bouncing back. A reading of 3:1three to one is poor, with about 25 percent bouncing back. If your SWR is 3:1three to one or higher, check the antenna and the feed line. Look for damage, water, or a loose link.

An SWR meter measures standing wave ratio. You place it between the radio and the feed line and send a low-power test signal. The meter shows the SWR. A directional wattmeter can also measure SWR. Check SWR when you set up a new antenna, and check it again if your signal gets weak. A change in SWR means something changed at the antenna or in the feed line. An antenna analyzer is a tool that checks if an antenna is resonant at the frequency you want.

The picture with this lesson shows how SWR works. It has two parts. The left part shows a good match. The radio sits on the left, and a feed line runs to the antenna on the right. Three arrows point from the radio toward the antenna. All the power goes to the antenna, and nothing comes back. The right part shows a poor match. One arrow points from the radio toward the antenna, but two arrows point back toward the radio. Power reflects back toward the radio, and reflected power can damage the radio.

An antenna tuner is a box that matches the antenna to the radio. It changes the impedance the radio sees, so it makes a bad match look good to the radio. It does not fix the antenna. The antenna still has the same SWR. But the radio stays safe, and it can send full power. A tuner helps when one antenna must work on more than one band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use.

An example

Say you put up a new dipole for the 2 meter band. A dipole is a simple antenna made of two straight wires. You link it to your radio with coax and place an SWR meter in the line. You send a short test signal, and the meter reads 1.2:1one point two to one. That is a good match. Nearly all your power reaches the antenna.

A week later, you test again. This time the meter reads 3:1three to one. Something changed. You check the feed line and find water in the coax connector. You dry it and seal it. The meter reads 1.2:1one point two to one again. The SWR meter found the fault fast. Without the meter, you would keep sending power into a bad match.

What to remember

  • A feed line carries signals between the radio and the antenna.
  • Coax has a center wire inside a shield.
  • SWR shows how well the antenna matches the feed line.
  • A reading of 1:1one to one is a perfect match.
  • A high SWR wastes power and can harm the radio.
  • High SWR makes a solid-state radio cut power to protect its transistors.
  • An antenna tuner protects the radio but does not fix the antenna.

Words to know

  • Feed line: the cable that carries radio signals between the radio and the antenna.
  • Coaxial cable (coax): a feed line with a center wire inside a shield.
  • Shield: a metal layer that keeps noise out and the signal in.
  • Standing wave ratio (SWR): a number that shows how well an antenna matches its feed line.
  • Impedance: the resistance a part shows to radio signals.
  • Ohm: the unit for resistance.
  • Reflected power: power that bounces back from the antenna toward the radio.
  • SWR meter: a meter that measures standing wave ratio.
  • directional wattmeter: a meter that can measure SWR.
  • antenna analyzer: a tool that checks if an antenna is resonant.
  • Antenna tuner: a box that matches the antenna to the radio.
  • Transmission line: another name for a feed line.
  • Load: the part that uses the power, here the antenna.
  • Dipole: a simple antenna made of two straight wires.
  • Band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.

Lesson 29: Connectors and cable

The last lesson covered feed lines and SWR. This one covers the parts that join the cable to your radio and antenna. You will learn which connector to use, which cable to pick, and how to keep them safe outdoors.

  • Which feed line has the lowest loss.

The idea in plain words

A connector joins a cable to a radio or an antenna. It must fit well and stay tight. A loose connector adds loss. It can also let water in.

The PL-259 is the most common ham connector. You will see it on most HF and VHF radios. HF means high frequency. VHF means very high frequency. A PL-259 screws onto its mate. But it is not watertight. It is not made for microwave use. Use it at HF and VHF.

A BNC connector is small. It locks with a push and a twist. That lock is called a bayonet lock. You will see BNC connectors on test gear and on some handheld radios.

A Type N connector is the best pick above 400 megahertz. It fits tight and keeps working at high frequencies. You will see Type N connectors on UHF gear. UHF means ultra high frequency. Above 400 megahertz, use Type N.

RG-58 and RG-213 are two common types of coax. RG-58 is thin and easy to bend. It is a good pick for short runs. RG-213 is thicker. At the same frequency, RG-213 has less loss than RG-58. Use RG-213 for long runs. It can also handle more power than RG-58.

The layer between the center wire and the shield is called the dielectric. Some coax uses foam as the dielectric. Foam has tiny air pockets in it. Air loses less signal than solid plastic. So foam cable loses less than solid cable.

The feed line with the lowest loss is air-insulated hardline. It is a stiff, thick cable. Air sits between the center wire and the shield instead of solid plastic. Hardline is hard to bend.

Water and sun damage cable. Tape every outdoor connector to keep water out. Tape PL-259, BNC, and Type N connectors. All of them need tape. Sunlight also harms cable. The sun's ultraviolet light cracks the outer coat over time. A cracked coat lets water in. Check outdoor cable for cracks.

An example

Say you set up a station on a band above 400 megahertz. You pick Type N connectors. You run RG-213 coax from the radio to the antenna. RG-213 has less loss than RG-58. So more of your signal gets to the antenna. You tape each outdoor connector to keep water out. A year later, you check the cable. The sun has not cracked the coat. The tape is still tight. Your signal is still strong.

  • Air-insulated hardline has the lowest loss of any feed line.
  • Tape all outdoor connectors to keep water out.
  • Sunlight can crack a cable's outer coat.

Lesson 30: Electrical safety and lightning

The last lesson covered connectors and cable.

What you will learn

  • How a fuse keeps you safe.
  • How to wire a station the safe way.
  • Why every ground rod must connect to the others.
  • What a lightning arrester does.
  • What to do when lightning is near.

The idea in plain words

Electricity can hurt you. Current is the flow of electrons through a wire. When current flows through your body, it heats your tissue. It can also upset the work of your cells. It can make your muscles tighten, and you may not let go. So treat every wire as if it carries current.

House power is alternating current (AC), which changes direction many times each second. Your house power is 120 volts. One wire is hot. The hot wire brings power from the source, and in the United States the black wire is the hot wire. One wire is neutral. The neutral wire carries current back to the power source. One wire is the ground wire. The ground wire is a safe path for electric current to flow into the Earth. Use three-wire cords and plugs for all your station gear. Connect all your AC powered gear to one common safety ground.

A fuse is a part that breaks the circuit if too much current flows. The fuse removes power when a circuit has an overload. An overload is more current than a circuit can safely carry. A fuse sits in series with the hot wire only. In series means the fuse is in the same path as the current. One warning: never replace a 5-ampere fuse with a 20-ampere fuse. Too much current can then flow, and it can start a fire. A circuit breaker is a switch that trips open when too much current flows. You can reset a circuit breaker, but you must replace a blown fuse.

Grounding gives stray current a safe path into the Earth. Every metal case in your station should connect to ground. Use one ground bus for all your gear: a metal bar that joins all the ground wires. Run one heavy wire from the bus to a ground rod outside. A ground rod is a metal rod driven deep into the Earth. A tower needs a separate eight-foot ground rod at each leg. Bond all your ground rods together with heavy wire. To bond means to join with a good metal-to-metal link. Bond the station ground to the house ground too. Your local electrical codes set the rules for grounding.

The picture with this lesson shows a station ground. At the left, a radio sits on a desk, with a power supply next to it. A power supply is a box that changes house power into the direct current (DC) power a radio needs. Direct current flows in one direction only. A short wire runs from each case to a ground bus under the desk. One heavy wire runs from the bus through the wall to a ground rod outside. An antenna is the metal part that sends out radio signals and picks them up. A feed line runs from the antenna to a panel where it enters the house. A feed line is the cable that carries radio signals between the radio and the antenna. A lightning arrester sits on that panel. A lightning arrester is a part that sends a lightning surge to ground. A surge is a short rush of too much power. The arrester connects to the same ground rod. All the ground rods join together with heavy wire.

Lightning seeks the tallest path to ground. So put a lightning arrester in each coaxial feed line. A coaxial cable is a feed line with a center wire inside a shield. Put the arrester on a grounded panel near where the feed lines enter the building. Keep all ground wires for lightning short and direct. Do not put sharp bends in them. When lightning is near, stop using the radio. When a storm is on its way, unplug your feed lines from the radio. Do not touch the radio, the feed lines, or the antenna during a storm.

Power lines can kill you. So look up before you raise any antenna, and keep the antenna far from all overhead wires. Plan so that a falling antenna stays at least 10 feet from all power wires. Never attach an antenna to a utility pole, which carries high-voltage power lines. Never climb a tower alone. Get training first, wear a harness, and stay tied to the tower at all times. Never climb a crank-up tower unless it is retracted, or mechanical safety locking devices have been installed. A turnbuckle pulls guy lines tight. A safety wire through a turnbuckle keeps it from loosening from vibration.

A 12-volt storage battery can push huge current if its terminals short. A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance. Shorting the terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion. Fast charging can make a battery overheat or give off gas. A power supply can still hurt you after you switch it off. Its filter capacitors still hold charge. A capacitor is a part that stores energy in an electric field. So let the charge drain before you work inside any gear. Use a meter rated for the voltage you will test.

An example

Say you set up your first station on a desk by a window. You run a short wire from the radio case to a ground bus. A heavy wire runs from the bus to a ground rod in the yard. Your feed line comes through the wall to a panel, and you put a lightning arrester on the panel. One night, a storm comes. You stop using the radio and unplug the feed line. Lightning hits near your house. The arrester sends the surge to the ground rod, and your radio stays safe. A week later, a wire inside the power supply shorts. The fuse blows at once. It cuts the power before the wire can get hot. You replace the fuse with one of the same size.

  • A fuse removes power when a circuit has an overload.

  • Bond all ground rods together with heavy wire.
  • Put a lightning arrester on a grounded panel where feed lines enter the house.
  • Keep antennas far from power lines.
  • Never climb a tower alone.
  • Unplug your feed lines when a storm is on its way.

  • circuit breaker: a switch that trips open when too much current flows.
  • overload: more current than a circuit can safely carry.
  • ground (grounding): a safe path for electric current to flow into the Earth.
  • ground rod: a metal rod driven deep into the Earth.
  • lightning arrester: a part that sends a lightning surge to ground.
  • short circuit: a path with almost no resistance.
  • turnbuckle: a part that pulls guy lines tight.

Lesson 31: RF exposure: staying safe around radio waves

The last lesson covered electrical safety and lightning.

What you will learn

  • What RF exposure means.
  • Why RF does not change your cells.
  • What sets the safe limit.
  • How distance and power change your exposure.
  • How to check your own station.

The idea in plain words

RF means radio frequency, the set of frequencies used for radio signals. Frequency is how many times a radio wave repeats in one second. RF exposure is the amount of radio energy that reaches a person's body. RF signals are a form of radiation. Radiation is energy that moves out from a source. RF signals are non-ionizing radiation. That means the waves do not have enough energy to change your cells. Ionizing radiation has enough energy to harm cells. Radio waves are not that kind.

RF can still heat your body, and that heat is the main risk from RF. Strong RF heats the tissue it reaches. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) sets limits on that heat. The limits cap how much RF may reach a person. The limits change with frequency. Your body takes in more RF at some frequencies than at others. The strictest limit is near 50 megahertz (MHz). A megahertz is one million hertz. One hertz is one repeat per second.

Four things set how much RF reaches you. The first is power. More power means more RF in the air. The second is distance. Stand far from an antenna, and less RF will reach you. An antenna is the metal part that sends out RF signals and picks them up. The third is the radiation pattern. A radiation pattern is the shape of the signal an antenna sends out. Some antennas send most of their signal in one direction. The fourth is duty cycle. Duty cycle is the percent of time that a transmitter sends. A transmitter is the part that sends signals out. Take a duty cycle of 50 percent: the transmitter sends half the time. Duty cycle sets your average exposure.

The FCC limits use power density. Power density is how much RF falls on one spot. A lower duty cycle raises the power density you may use. A duty cycle of 50 percent helps you: it lets you use twice the power density of full-time sending.

You can lower the RF you take in, and here is how. Move the antenna farther from people. That is the best step you can take. Use the lowest power that gets the job done. Keep people away when you transmit. Never touch an antenna while you transmit. Touching it can burn your skin. An RF burn is a burn from RF. The station licensee has the duty to keep people safe. The station licensee is the person whose name is on the license. That person must make sure no one gets more RF than the FCC limits allow.

Check your station before you use it. You can work out the numbers with FCC tables. The tables are in OET Bulletin 65. OET stands for the Office of Engineering and Technology. You can also use a computer model. A model is a program that works out the numbers for you. You can also measure the signal strength with calibrated gear. Calibrated means tested for true readings. Check the station again each time you change the transmitter or the antenna. New gear or more power can change your exposure. Keep a note of your check. Write down the power, the antenna, and the safe distance.

An example

Say you put a new antenna on your roof. You run 100 watts of power. A watt is the unit for power. You check the FCC tables for your band. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for one use. The tables say people must stay 10 feet away. But your back porch is only 6 feet below it. So you move the antenna to the far end of the roof. Now the porch is 15 feet away. You check the tables again, and the numbers now pass. You also cut your power to 50 watts when you talk to friends nearby. Less power means less RF for all.

What to remember

  • Radio signals are non-ionizing radiation.
  • Strong RF heats the tissue it reaches.
  • Distance, power, pattern, and duty cycle set your exposure.
  • Move the antenna farther from people to cut exposure.
  • Never touch an antenna while you transmit.
  • Check your station with the FCC tables or a meter.
  • Check again each time you change the transmitter or the antenna.
  • The station licensee must keep all people within the limits.

  • radiation pattern: the shape of the signal an antenna sends out.
  • transmitter: the part that sends signals out.
  • exposure limit: the most RF that may reach a person.
  • power density: how much RF falls on one spot.
  • megahertz (MHz): one million hertz.
  • band: a range of frequencies set aside for one use.
  • watt: the unit for power.

The idea in plain words

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) gives each ham a free number for license paperwork. That number is your FRN. FRN means FCC Registration Number. You need it before the exam. You get it from CORES (Commission Registration System). CORES is the FCC's online system for license paperwork. It costs nothing. Write the number down and bring it to the exam.

Next, find an exam session. A session is a set time and place to take the exam. Many teams run sessions in person. They meet in libraries, club rooms, and halls. You can find a session with the American Radio Relay League (ARRL) exam search. Some teams run sessions by video. A remote session runs over video, and at least 3 volunteer examiners watch it live. A volunteer examiner is a licensed ham who gives exams. You need a computer camera. You show the room with the camera. This is a room scan. A helper can do the room scan and then leave.

There are two fees. The first fee is the session fee. A Volunteer Examiner Coordinator (VEC) sets it. A VEC is a group that runs license exam sessions. The ARRL VEC charges 15 dollars per session, and 5 dollars for a candidate under 18. Other VECs charge 10 to 15 dollars or nothing. Ask the team what it charges. The second fee is the FCC fee. It is 35 dollars for a new license. You pay it to the FCC in CORES within 10 calendar days after the FCC files your application. If you miss the 10 days, the FCC drops your application. You can file it again without a new exam. Your pass stays valid for 365 days.

Bring a photo identification (ID), the session fee, and your FRN. The team checks your ID and gives you the exam. The exam has 35 questions. Each question has 4 choices. You pass with 26 right. Most teams let you try the next license in the same session after you pass. When you pass, you get a paper called a CSCE. CSCE means Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination. It is a paper that proves you passed an exam element. Keep it safe. It stays valid for 365 days.

Take a full practice exam before exam day. A Facebook quiz cannot hold 35 questions, but a full practice exam shows if you are ready.

Ask for help in advance. The law says the examiners must help you. The rule is in the Code of Federal Regulations, 47 CFR 97.509(k). It says the examiners must accommodate an examinee whose physical disabilities require a special examination procedure. You can ask for the questions read aloud, an exam with no figures, a Braille exam, or extra time. Ask before you book. The team must approve it first. Nothing can be arranged on exam day. The team may ask for a note from your doctor, but only if your need is not clear. For most members, an in-person session is the better choice. The room scan and camera work in a remote session are visual tasks. They are hard without sight. So ask for help in advance at an in-person session.

After you pass, the team files your application. Pay the 35 dollar FCC fee in CORES within 10 calendar days. Then watch your email. The FCC sends an email with a link to download your license grant. Your license also appears in the FCC database. The database is ULS. ULS means Universal Licensing System. That listing proves the FCC issued your license. Your license lasts 10 years. Renewal costs 35 dollars, and it needs no new exam.

Think of a ham named Sam. Sam reads all 35 lessons and takes each short quiz. He takes full practice exams. He gets his FRN from CORES and finds a session near his home. He asks for the questions read aloud, and the team approves it. He brings his photo ID and the session fee. He passes with 30 right and gets his CSCE. He pays the 35 dollar FCC fee. His call sign comes by email. Then he joins the Saturday net as a licensed ham. That could be you.

Lesson 33: Talking through satellites

You know how to run a station and talk on local bands. Now let's aim your signal at the sky. Hams have their own satellites, and you can talk through one with a small radio.

What you will learn

  • How ham radio satellites work, and how you talk through one.
  • What Doppler shift does to a satellite signal.
  • How to hear a satellite beacon and match its strength.
  • What you need to work a satellite pass.
  • The difference between FM satellites and linear transponders.

The idea in plain words

Hams have their own satellites. Most fly in low Earth orbit, or LEO. They circle the Earth in about 100 minutes. They move fast. A pass lasts just a few minutes. You need to know when to listen. That's what tracking programs do. You feed them the Keplerian elements, the numbers that describe the orbit. Then they tell you when the pass starts, where to point your antenna, and how high it will rise. They even give you the frequency. They also show maps of the satellite track.

To talk through a satellite, you send your signal up on one band. The satellite sends it back down on another. Your signal up is the uplink, and the signal back down is the downlink. A common setup is U/V mode. You send up on the 70 centimeter band, and it sends you back down on 2 meters. Satellites use many modes. SSB, FM, and CW or data all work. Use the mode you know.

Here's the tricky part: Doppler shift. The satellite moves fast toward you, then away from you. That motion changes the frequency you hear. As it comes toward you, the signal sounds higher than the listed frequency. As it moves away, it sounds lower. So you keep tuning a little as the pass goes on. Your tracking program tells you how much. After a pass or two, it feels natural.

Satellites also send beacons. A beacon is a signal the satellite sends on its own. It carries telemetry, data about the satellite's health and status. Anyone can listen to telemetry. The beacon helps when you transmit, too. On a linear transponder satellite, your signal coming back down should sound about as strong as the beacon. If yours is much louder, turn your power down. Too much power on the uplink blocks other users.

One more thing you'll notice on a pass: spin fading. The satellite turns slowly as it flies. Its antennas turn with it. So the signal fades in and out as the pass goes on. That's normal. It's not a fault in your radio.

You do not need a big station to work a satellite. A handheld radio and a small directional antenna are enough to start. Many hams make their first satellite contact with the same radio they use for local repeaters. Listen first. Hear the beacon, then hear other hams call. Call when the pass is high in the sky. That is when the signal is strongest and the pass lasts longest.

Satellites come in two main kinds. An FM satellite works like a repeater in the sky. It hears on one channel and sends back on one channel. Only one ham talks at a time. A linear transponder satellite is different. It takes in a whole slice of band and sends it back down. Many hams use it at once, mostly on SSB and CW. The beacon helps you set your power on this kind. Match the beacon, and you will not block other users.

The space station counts too. The International Space Station carries a ham repeater. You can talk through it when it passes over. You can also just listen. Anyone can hear satellite telemetry. Free software on a computer can turn the beeps into words and numbers. You learn a lot by listening before you ever send.

A space station is an amateur station more than 50 km above Earth's surface. Any amateur allowed to transmit on the satellite uplink frequency can be the control operator. You can run a station that works a satellite or space station.

An example

Sam is new, and she wants to try a satellite. She checks a tracking program on her phone. It shows a pass coming over her town at 18:30. She steps outside with her handheld radio and a small directional antenna. First she hears the beacon. Then she hears other hams calling. She tunes a little as the satellite moves. Then she makes her first contact — a ham two states away, through a satellite. The whole pass lasts eight minutes, and she's hooked.

What to remember

  • Most ham satellites circle Earth in about 100 minutes.
  • You send up on one band and hear back down on another. U/V mode is up on 70 centimeters, down on 2 meters.
  • Doppler shift changes the frequency you hear, so keep tuning during a pass.
  • A beacon sends the satellite's health and status. Anyone can listen to it.
  • Too much power on the uplink blocks other users, so match the beacon's strength.
  • An FM satellite is one channel at a time. A linear transponder shares a slice of band.
  • A space station is an amateur station more than 50 km above Earth's surface.

Words to know

  • LEO (Low Earth Orbit): an orbit close to Earth. A satellite circles in about 100 minutes.
  • uplink: the signal you send up to the satellite.
  • downlink: the signal the satellite sends back down to you.
  • Doppler shift: a change in the frequency you hear as a satellite moves toward or away from you.
  • beacon: a signal a satellite sends on its own, with its health and status.
  • telemetry: data a satellite sends about its own health and status.
  • Keplerian elements: numbers that describe a satellite's orbit. Tracking programs use them to predict passes.
  • spin fading: signal fading caused by the satellite turning as it flies.
  • transponder: a device on a satellite that hears a slice of band and sends it back down.
  • pass: one trip of a satellite over your sky, from rise to set.
  • FM satellite: a satellite that works like a repeater in the sky, one channel at a time.
  • Space station: an amateur station more than 50 km above Earth's surface.

Lesson 34: Finding signals, contests, and linking over the internet

You can talk through a satellite now. Back on Earth, hams also hunt for signals, race in contests, and link radios over the internet. This lesson covers all three.

What you will learn

  • How to find a hidden transmitter and track down noise.
  • What a contest is, and how you take part.
  • How IRLP, EchoLink, and AllStarLink link radios over the internet.

The idea in plain words

Now let's come back down to Earth. Direction finding means finding where a signal comes from. Hams do it with a directional antenna, one that hears best in one direction. You turn it until the signal is strongest, and that tells you which way to look. It's the same skill you use to track down noise or jamming. Walk around with that antenna and follow the signal to its source. Hams also turn it into a game: a hidden transmitter hunt, or fox hunt. Someone hides a small transmitter. Everyone else tries to find it first. All you need is a radio and that antenna.

Start wide, then narrow down. First find the rough direction with your antenna. Then walk toward the signal. Your own body can block the signal, and that helps too. Turn until the signal fades. The weak spot points away from the source. A fox hunt is good practice. It teaches the same skill you need to find noise.

Contests are another kind of game. They're a great way to build skill fast. In a contest, you contact as many stations as you can in a set time. Keep it short. Send only the minimum: your call sign and the contest exchange. Don't call the same station twice to check you're in their log. Don't sign with just the last two letters of your call. Some contests use grid locators. These codes mark a place on Earth with a letter and a number.

Each contest has its own exchange. The exchange is the short bit of facts you swap. It is often your call sign and a signal report. Some contests ask for your grid locator or your state. Log each contact as you go. A log is your list of contacts. Send your log in after the contest ends. The rules tell you how and when.

The last idea in this lesson is internet linking. IRLP, the Internet Radio Linking Project, links repeaters over the internet. You reach an IRLP node with DTMF tones from your keypad. DTMF sends two simultaneous audio tones. EchoLink goes a step further. It lets you send through a repeater with no radio to start the contact. To use EchoLink, first register your call sign and show proof of your license. Both systems use VoIP: voice sent over the internet using digital signals. A station that links others to the internet is a gateway. A linked repeater network joins several repeaters. A signal heard by one repeater goes out on all of them.

AllStarLink is a third way to link over the internet. It links repeaters and nodes the same way IRLP does. Many hams use it now. It is the biggest linking network, with more than 10,000 nodes on the air. You can join it with an app on your phone. You do not need your own node to listen or talk.

A list of the apps for EchoLink and AllStarLink sits in the 'Get the software' section below.

  • In a linked repeater network, a signal heard by one repeater goes out on all of them.
  • AllStarLink links repeaters over the internet, and it is the biggest linking network.

Lesson 35: Digital voice and more digital modes

You've reached the last lesson, and it's a good one. You know voice modes, Morse code, and the first digital modes. Now let's meet the rest: digital voice, typing modes, pictures by radio, and email without the internet.

What you will learn

  • What DMR is, and how talkgroups, code plugs, and color codes work.
  • What D-STAR is, and what you must do before you send.
  • How PSK and RTTY send typed words by radio.
  • How WSJT-X helps with signals too weak to hear.
  • How hams send pictures with slow-scan and fast-scan TV.
  • How Winlink sends email by radio, and how mesh networks link stations.

The idea in plain words

Most voice on the radio is analog. Your voice moves the signal up and down. Digital voice is different. Your radio turns your voice into numbers. Then it sends those numbers. The other radio turns the numbers back into your voice. It sounds clear, even when the signal is weak. Two digital voice modes are on the exam: DMR and D-STAR.

DMR is short for Digital Mobile Radio. It is a digital voice mode. It packs two voice talks onto one 12.5 kilohertz repeater channel by taking turns very fast. That is why the exam calls it time multiplexing. Hams pick a talkgroup to join a group talk. A talkgroup is an ID number for a group of stations. You pick the group by entering its ID code. A code plug is the file of settings you load into a DMR radio. It holds the repeaters and talkgroups you use. A color code is the access code for a DMR repeater. You must program it into your radio before the repeater will hear you.

D-STAR is a digital voice mode too. It works through repeaters linked over the internet. You must put your call sign in the radio before you send. The radio sends your call sign with your voice. That is how the network knows who you are.

A hotspot is a small device that links your radio to a digital voice network over the internet. You use it at home. It lets your handheld reach the whole world through DMR or D-STAR. You need no big antenna. The internet does the long haul.

Some modes send typed words, not voice. PSK is short for Phase Shift Keying. It is a digital mode that sends data by shifting the phase of the signal. Hams use it to type to each other on HF. PSK31 is the common form. It is narrow, so it fits where voice will not. RTTY is short for radioteletype. It is an old digital mode that sends typed words by radio. Hams still use it in contests. It is wider and louder than PSK. But it is simple, and it works.

WSJT-X is free software for weak-signal digital modes. Hams use it for FT8, which you met in Lesson 25. They also use it for moonbounce, meteor scatter, and weak signal beacons. These are signals too weak to hear by ear. The software pulls them out of the noise. It turns faint traces into words on your screen.

Hams send pictures by radio too. Slow-scan TV sends still pictures. It takes a minute or more to send one picture. Each picture comes in line by line. You watch it build on your screen. Fast-scan TV sends moving pictures, like broadcast TV. It takes about 6 megahertz of space. That is much wider than voice. It uses the same kind of signal old broadcast TV used. NTSC is the name for that old analog color TV signal.

Winlink is a system that sends email by radio. Each ham has an email address based on their call sign. It works when the internet is down. That makes it useful in an emergency. A mesh network is a data network hams build with Wi-Fi gear. The gear runs special firmware. Each station passes data to the next. A mesh can carry email, files, and voice across a town with no internet at all.

An example

Sam buys a DMR handheld. She loads a code plug from her club's website. It holds the local repeaters and talkgroups. She programs the color code for her nearest repeater. Then she picks a talkgroup for her state. She presses the talk button and calls. A ham three counties away answers. They talk for a few minutes. Sam is hooked on digital voice.

That evening, Sam tries slow-scan TV. She tunes to the calling frequency and hears the warbling tones. A picture builds on her screen, line by line. It is a photo of a ham's garden. She saves it and smiles. Radio can carry pictures too.

  • D-STAR needs your call sign in the radio before you send.
  • PSK sends typed words by shifting the phase. RTTY is the older typing mode.
  • WSJT-X is free software for weak-signal modes like FT8.
  • Slow-scan TV sends still pictures. Fast-scan TV sends moving pictures and takes about 6 megahertz.
  • Winlink sends email by radio, and it works when the internet is down.
  • A mesh network links stations with Wi-Fi gear, each passing data to the next.

WSJT-X is built for very weak signals, and it has modes for all three of these jobs. Moonbounce, beacons, and meteor scatter all use signals too faint for voice.

Question 10. What is meant by the term "NTSC?"

Pool question T8D04

NTSC is the old analog color television standard used in North America. Hams use it for fast-scan TV, sending live moving pictures over the air.

Question 11. Which of the following relays messages using email addresses based on amateur callsigns?

Pool question T2C08

Winlink moves email over radio, and each ham gets an address based on their call sign. It is a handy way to send messages when the internet is down.

Question 12. Which of the following best describes an amateur radio mesh network?

Pool question T8D12

A mesh network links ordinary Wi-Fi radios that have been reprogrammed for ham frequencies. Each station passes data along, so the network keeps working even if one link drops.

Get the software — EchoLink and AllStarLink.

You need an app or a program to use EchoLink and AllStarLink. Pick the one that fits your phone or computer. All of them need a valid amateur license. EchoLink and AllStarLink both ask for proof of license when you sign up.

EchoLink

  • EchoLink for Windows is free. Get it at echolink.org.
  • EchoLink is free on the App Store for iPhone and iPad.
  • EchoLink is free on Google Play for Android.
  • EchoLink Web runs in your browser. Nothing to install. Go to webapp.echolink.org.
  • EchoHam works with EchoLink on a Mac. Find it on the Mac App Store.

AllStarLink

  • RepeaterPhone costs $7.99 once. It talks to EchoLink and AllStarLink. It was built with help from VoiceOver users.
  • OpLine costs $6.99 once. It talks to AllStarLink only. It also runs on a Mac.
  • Transceive is for Mac. It connects to AllStarLink nodes, up to eight at once, and lets you manage your linked nodes. It costs 19 dollars and 99 cents, once.
  • DVSwitch Mobile is free. It talks to AllStarLink. It can also reach DMR and other digital modes.
  • DroidStar is free. It talks to AllStarLink and to DMR, D-STAR, and more.
  • You can run your own AllStarLink node on a Raspberry Pi or a computer. The software is free at allstarlink.org. You do not need your own node to use the apps above.

RepeaterPhone and OpLine do not run on Android. Android users pick DVSwitch Mobile or DroidStar.

Digital voice

  • Peanut is free. It talks to D-STAR, DMR, Fusion, and M17 rooms from your phone or computer. You need a Peanut code, free from the Peanut site, and a license.

Radio Programming Software Options

To put channels into a radio from a computer, you need a programming program. The program depends on the radio. Most radios use CHIRP or RT Systems. Radios that run accessible firmware do not use CHIRP or RT Systems. Each of those uses its own program, or the radio maker's program, as listed below.

CHIRP (CHIRP-next)

CHIRP is free and open source. It runs on Windows, Mac, and Linux. It programs hundreds of radio models from many makers. It can read a channel list from a CSV file, which is how the VIH frequency files are built to work with it. Get it only from the official site, because other sites may carry old or changed copies.

  • CHIRP download page

RT Systems

RT Systems sells one programming program for each radio model. You buy the program made for your exact radio. It runs on Windows or Mac, depending on the radio. It also sells the programming cable for each radio. JAWS scripts for RT Systems programs are listed on the blind hams links page below. RT Systems is a paid program, not free.

  • RT Systems website

OpenGD77 CPS

OpenGD77 is free firmware with voice prompts, made by Roger Clark, VK3KYY, with help from Daniel, F1RMB, and others. It runs on the Radioddity GD-77 and GD-77S, the Baofeng DM-1801 and RD-5R, the TYT MD-UV380 and Retevis RT-3S, the Baofeng DM-1701 and Retevis RT-84, the TYT MD-9600 and Retevis RT-90, and the TYT MD2017 and Retevis RT-82. The OpenGD77 CPS is the programming program for these radios. Do not use CHIRP or RT Systems on a radio that runs OpenGD77. The OpenGD77 forum closed in 2026, and the community forum moved to opengd77.org. Downloads still work at the address below.

  • OpenGD77 downloads page

Accessible GD77

Accessible GD77 is Joe Stephen's, VK7JS, accessible build of OpenGD77, made for blind operators. It comes in a stable version and a beta test version. The stable version is the one most people want. The beta version is for testing new work. There is a mailing list for news about Accessible GD77. The mailing list link below did not load when checked on 2026-11-23, so it may have moved.

  • Stable version: Accessible GD77 stable version
  • Mailing list: Accessible GD77 mailing list
  • Blind hams links page, which lists the stable and beta versions and more.

OpenRTX

OpenRTX is free and open source firmware, started in March 2020 by Niccolo Izzo, IU2KIN, Silvano Seva, IU2KWO, Federico Amedeo Izzo, IU2NUO, and Frederik Saraci, IU2NRO, in Italy. It has voice prompts for vision impaired operators, added in November 2022. It runs on the TYT MD-380 and MD-390, the TYT MD-UV380 and MD-UV390, the Retevis RT3 and RT3S, the Radioddity GD-77, the Baofeng DM-1801 and DM-1701, the Connect Systems CS7000-M17 and CS7000-M17 Plus, and others. It does FM and M17 digital voice. It does not do DMR. The firmware is loaded with the radio maker's update tool, the radio_tool program on Linux, or a web flasher. NOTE, TO CONFIRM: a current reference says that channel lists, called codeplugs, still need the radio maker's own software. OpenRTX does not have its own channel programming program.   • OpenRTX website
